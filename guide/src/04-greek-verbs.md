# greek-verbs | Greek verbs

A Greek verb packs a lot into one word: what happens, who does it, how the action is viewed, whether it is a statement, a wish or a command, and whether the subject does it or has it done to them. This section takes those pieces one at a time.

## greek-verb | How a Greek verb is built
lang: grc
aka: Greek verb, verb parts, parsing a verb, conjugation, conjugate
see: verb, tense, aspect, voice, mood, person, number, augment, reduplication
lessons: grc-4, grc-5
short: A Greek verb is a stem with pieces added: often something at the front for the past, then the stem, then an ending that shows who is doing it.

Take ἐπιστεύσαμεν, “we believed”. It breaks into ἐ- (a mark of the past, the [[augment]]), πιστευ- (the stem, “believe”), -σα- (the sign of the [[aorist]]) and -μεν (“we”). Each piece tells you something, and the grammar label lists what they add up to:

- [[tense]]: [[present]], [[imperfect-greek|imperfect]], [[future]], [[aorist]], [[perfect-greek|perfect]] or [[pluperfect]], which shows the [[aspect]] (and, in statements, the time);
- [[voice]]: [[active]], [[middle]] or [[passive]];
- [[mood]]: [[indicative]], [[subjunctive]], [[optative]], [[imperative]], or one of the forms that are not moods, the [[infinitive]] and [[participle]];
- [[person]] and [[number]]: who is doing it.

So “Verb aorist active indicative 1st plural” describes ἐπιστεύσαμεν exactly. A participle's label gives a [[case]], number and gender instead of a person, because it works like an adjective.

You don't need to work all this out alone: every word in the app has its label. But knowing what the pieces are lets you see why the English is what it is.

## present | Present
lang: grc
aka: present tense, pres., present indicative
match: present=an action seen as going on: present
see: aspect, imperfect-greek, tense
lessons: grc-4
find: grc ^V-P
short: The verb form for an action seen as going on or repeated. In a statement it usually means now: λύω, “I loose” or “I am loosing”.

The present views an action from the inside, as it unfolds, or as something that happens again and again. In statements it usually refers to now: λέγει, “he says”, or “he is saying”. In commands it often means “keep doing”: see [[imperative]].

Greek storytellers, especially Mark and John, sometimes switch into the present in the middle of a past story to make it vivid: καὶ λέγει αὐτοῖς, “and he says to them”. Grammar books call this the historic present, and [[translations|English translations]] usually turn it back into the past.

## imperfect-greek | Imperfect
lang: grc
aka: imperfect tense, impf., Greek imperfect
match: imperfect=an action going on in the past: “was doing”
see: present, aorist, augment, aspect
find: grc ^V-I
short: The past form of the present: an action going on, repeated or begun in the past: “he was teaching”, “he used to teach”, “he began to teach”.

The imperfect takes the [[present]]'s view of an action (from the inside, as ongoing) and sets it in the past. It is made from the present stem with an [[augment]] at the front: ἐδίδασκεν, “he was teaching”, from διδάσκω.

English can translate it in several ways depending on the context: “he was teaching”, “he kept teaching”, “he used to teach” or “he began to teach”. In stories, the imperfect often paints the background while the [[aorist]] tells the main events.

Hebrew grammar uses the same word, imperfect, for a quite different form: see [[imperfect-hebrew]].

## future | Future
unattested: ἄξω
lang: grc
aka: future tense, fut., shall, will
match: future=an action still to come; 2nd future=a future formed without the usual σ
see: present, aorist, tense
find: grc ^V-2?F
short: The form for what will happen: ὄψεσθε, “you will see”. It usually adds σ to the stem.

The Greek future is the one form that is mainly about time. It is usually made by adding σ before the ending: σώσει, “he will save”, from σῴζω. When the stem ends in a consonant, the σ combines with it: ἄξω, from ἄγω. Some verbs form their future without the σ, or from a different stem, and STEPBible labels some of these “2nd future”: see [[second-aorist]].

The future can also give a solemn command, especially in quotations from the Old Testament: οὐ φονεύσεις, “you shall not murder”.

## aorist | Aorist
unattested: ἀόριστος, ἔλυσα
lang: grc
aka: aorist tense, aor., simple past, point action
match: aorist=an action seen as a whole event
see: aspect, second-aorist, imperfect-greek, augment, tense
find: grc ^V-A
short: The form that presents an action simply, as a whole event. In a statement it usually means the past: ἐπίστευσεν, “he believed”.

The aorist is the most common past form in Greek stories. It views an action from outside, as a single whole, without saying whether it took a moment or a long time. “Jesus wept”, ἐδάκρυσεν, is aorist.

In the [[indicative]] the aorist has an [[augment]] and usually means the past. In the other moods it has no augment and says nothing about time: an aorist [[imperative]] or [[subjunctive]] simply presents the action as a whole: πίστευσον, “believe!”.

The word comes from Greek ἀόριστος, “undefined”: the form doesn't define how the action unfolded. It is not a special “once for all” [[tense]], though a writer may of course describe a once-for-all event with it.

Most aorists add σα to the stem: ἔλυσα, “I loosed”. Many common verbs form it differently, without the σ: see [[second-aorist]].

## second-aorist | 2nd aorist (and other “2nd” forms)
lang: grc
aka: 2nd aorist, second aorist, strong aorist, 2nd perfect, 2nd future, 2nd pluperfect, irregular aorist
match: 2nd aorist=an aorist formed from a different stem, without σ; 2nd perfect=a perfect formed without the usual κ; 2nd pluperfect=a pluperfect formed without the usual κ
see: aorist, future, perfect-greek, principal-parts
find: grc ^V-2A
short: An [[aorist]] made in an older way, from a changed stem and without σ: ἔλαβον, “I took”, from λαμβάνω. It means exactly the same as any aorist.

The “2nd” is about how the form is made, not what it means. Some of the commonest verbs have a 2nd aorist: ἔρχομαι, “I come”, has ἦλθον, “I came”; λαμβάνω has ἔλαβον; βάλλω has ἔβαλον; and λέγω has εἶπον, “I said”.

These forms have the [[augment]], but instead of -σα- they put the endings of the [[imperfect-greek|imperfect]] on a different stem. So ἔβαλλον (imperfect, “I was throwing”) and ἔβαλον (2nd aorist, “I threw”) differ by one letter. This is why dictionaries list the main forms of each verb: see [[principal-parts]].

STEPBible's labels also mark “2nd perfect”, “2nd pluperfect” and “2nd future” for the same reason: forms made without the usual sign (κ for the [[perfect-greek|perfect]], σ for the future). Under each word the app shortens all of these to plain “aor.”, “perf.” and so on, because the meaning is the same.

> JHN 1:11 ἦλθεν | ἦλθεν, “he came”: the 2nd aorist of ἔρχομαι, whose present looks nothing like it

## perfect-greek | Perfect
unattested: λέλυκα
lang: grc
aka: perfect tense, perf., Greek perfect, stative
match: perfect=a finished action whose result still stands
see: reduplication, pluperfect, aspect, second-aorist
find: grc ^V-2?R
short: The form for a finished action whose result still stands: γέγραπται, “it is written” (it was written and stands written).

The perfect looks back at a completed action from the state it has left. τετέλεσται, “it is finished” ([JHN 19:30]), means the work has been completed and stays completed. γέγραπται, “it is written”, introduces a scripture that was written and still stands.

Most perfects start with [[reduplication]], a doubling of the first sound with ε: λέλυκα, “I have loosed”, πεπίστευκα, “I have believed”. Many add κ before the ending. English often translates the perfect with “has” or “have”, but sometimes with a plain present: οἶδα, a perfect in form, means “I know”.

## pluperfect | Pluperfect
lang: grc
aka: pluperfect tense, plupf., had done, past perfect
match: pluperfect=a finished action whose result stood at a past time
see: perfect-greek, augment, reduplication
find: grc ^V-2?L
short: The past of the [[perfect-greek|perfect]]: a result that stood at some time in the past: “he had come”, “it had been founded”.

The pluperfect is rare. It sets the state of a [[perfect-greek|perfect]] in the past: “the house did not fall, for it *had been founded* on the rock” ([MAT 7:25]). It usually has [[reduplication]], and sometimes an [[augment]] as well. Its endings have ει in them: τεθεμελίωτο.

## augment | Augment: the mark of the past
unattested: ἔλυον, ἤγειρα
lang: grc
aka: augment, ε-, past marker, syllabic augment, temporal augment
see: aorist, imperfect-greek, pluperfect, indicative
short: An ε added to the front of a verb, or a lengthened first vowel, that marks past time in the [[indicative]]: λύω, ἔλυον, “I was loosing”.

The augment is a sign of the past, and only in the indicative: the [[imperfect-greek|imperfect]], [[aorist]] and [[pluperfect]]. When a verb begins with a consonant, ε is added: βλέπω, ἔβλεψα, “I saw”. When it begins with a vowel, that vowel is lengthened instead: ἀκούω, ἤκουσα, “I heard”; ἐγείρω, ἤγειρα.

With verbs that have a [[preposition]] at the front, the augment goes after it, between it and the verb: ἐκβάλλω, ἐξέβαλεν, “he drove out”. Finding the augment helps you find the stem to look up.

## reduplication | Reduplication
unattested: λέλυκα
lang: grc
aka: doubling, reduplicated, perfect stem
see: perfect-greek, pluperfect
short: Doubling the first consonant of a verb with ε, the mark of the [[perfect-greek|perfect]]: λύω, λέλυκα, “I have loosed”.

Reduplication means “doubling again”. To make the [[perfect-greek|perfect]], Greek repeats the verb's first consonant with ε in front of the stem: πιστεύω, πεπίστευκα; γράφω, γέγραφα. When the verb starts with a vowel, the vowel is lengthened instead, as with the [[augment]].

Unlike the augment, reduplication stays in every mood, so it marks a perfect wherever it appears, including in [[participle|participles]]: ὁ γεγεννημένος, “the one who has been born”.

## middle | Middle
lang: grc
aka: middle voice, mid., middle deponent
match: middle=the subject acts for itself, or is involved in the action; middle deponent=middle in form, often active in meaning
see: voice, active, passive, middle-or-passive, deponent, reflexive
find: grc ^V-2?[PIFARL][MD]
short: The voice in which the subject is involved in its own action, often doing it for itself or to itself: ἀπεκρίνατο, “he answered”.

English has active (“I wash”) and passive (“I am washed”). Greek also has the middle, which says the subject is involved in the action in a closer way than usual: doing it for itself, to itself, or with its own interests in view. ἐνίψατο, “he washed himself” or “he washed his own (hands)”.

Often the difference is hard to bring into English, and many verbs simply have their meaning in the middle form: ἔρχομαι, “I come”, γίνομαι, “I become”, πορεύομαι, “I go”. These are the verbs older grammars call [[deponent]].

## middle-or-passive | Middle or passive
unattested: λύομαι
lang: grc
aka: middle/passive, mid./pass., middle or passive voice
match: middle or passive=one form serving for both middle and passive; middle or passive deponent=one form for both voices, often active in meaning
see: middle, passive, deponent
find: grc ^V-[PIRL]E
short: A form that could be either middle or passive, because in four tenses the two voices share their endings.

In the [[present]], [[imperfect-greek|imperfect]], [[perfect-greek|perfect]] and [[pluperfect]], the [[middle]] and [[passive]] endings are identical: λύομαι can be “I loose (for myself)” or “I am loosed”. Only the context shows which. STEPBible's labels say “middle or passive” where they have not chosen between them, and the app shortens it to “mid./pass.”.

In the [[aorist]] and [[future]], the passive has its own endings with θη in them: ἐλύθη, “he was loosed”.

## deponent | Deponent
lang: grc
aka: deponent verb, middle-only verb, passive deponent, middle deponent, dep.
match: deponent=middle or passive in form, active in meaning; passive deponent=passive in form, often active in meaning
see: middle, middle-or-passive, voice
find: grc ^V-2?[PIFARL][DON]
short: A verb with [[middle-or-passive|middle or passive]] endings but an active meaning, such as ἔρχομαι, “I come”. Many scholars now prefer to call these middle verbs.

ἔρχομαι, “I come”, has the endings of the [[middle]] or [[passive]], but it doesn't mean “I am come”. Older grammars said such verbs had “laid aside” (Latin *deponere*) their active forms, and called them deponent. STEPBible's labels use that word: “middle deponent”, “passive deponent”, “middle or passive deponent”.

Many scholars today think the label is misleading. These verbs mostly describe things in which the subject is closely involved: coming and going, becoming, feeling, thinking, receiving. That is just what the middle voice expresses, so they are simply middle verbs. For reading, the point is the same either way: translate them as active.

## subjunctive | Subjunctive
unattested: λύωμεν, λύομεν
lang: grc
aka: subjunctive mood, subj., may, might, should, let us
match: subjunctive=what may or should happen: possibility, purpose, “let us”
see: mood, indicative, prohibition, negative
find: grc ^V-2?[PIFARL][A-Z]S-
short: The form of a verb for what may or should happen, rather than what does: after “so that”, “if”, “whoever”, and for “let us…”.

The subjunctive is recognised by its long vowels, ω and η, in the ending: λύωμεν, “let us loose”, against λύομεν, “we loose”. It has no [[augment]], and in the subjunctive the [[aorist]] and [[present]] differ only in [[aspect]], not in time.

Its main uses:

- purpose, after ἵνα, “so that”: ἵνα πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων… μὴ ἀπόληται, “so that everyone who believes… should not perish” ([JHN 3:16]);
- conditions and “whoever” [[sentence-parts|clauses]] with ἐάν or ἄν: ἐὰν ὁμολογῶμεν, “if we confess”;
- “let us”, in the 1st person plural: ἀγαπῶμεν ἀλλήλους, “let us love one another”;
- “do not”, with μή and the aorist: see [[prohibition]];
- the strongest “never”, οὐ μή: “he will never die”.

## optative | Optative
lang: grc
aka: optative mood, opt., wish, may it be
match: optative=a wish or a remote possibility: “may it be”
see: mood, subjunctive
find: grc ^V-2?[PIFARL][A-Z]O-
short: A rare form for wishes and remote possibilities: μὴ γένοιτο, “may it never be!”.

The optative was dying out in New Testament times, and appears in only a handful of verses. Its endings contain οι or αι: γένοιτο, “may it be”. Paul's famous μὴ γένοιτο, “by no means!”, literally “may it not happen”, is an optative. Luke also uses it in polite or uncertain questions: “what this might be”.

## greek-infinitive | Greek infinitives
unattested: λύειν
lang: grc
aka: infinitive in Greek, to-form in Greek, articular infinitive
see: infinitive, case, accusative
find: grc ^V-2?[PIFARL][A-Z]N
short: The Greek “to” form of a verb: λύειν, “to loose”. It ends in -ειν, -σαι, -ναι or -σθαι, and is often used where English needs a whole clause.

The commonest endings of the [[infinitive]] are -ειν ([[present]] active, λύειν), -σαι ([[aorist]] active, λῦσαι), -ναι (εἶναι, “to be”) and -σθαι ([[middle]] or [[passive]], ἔρχεσθαι, “to come”). The infinitive has a [[tense]] and a [[voice]] but no [[person]].

Besides “to go” after verbs like “want” or “be able”, Greek uses the infinitive in many ways English doesn't:

- with “the” in front, as a noun: τὸ ζῆν Χριστός, “to live is Christ” ([PHP 1:21]);
- after a preposition with “the”, for time or reason: ἐν τῷ σπείρειν, “while he was sowing”;
- with its own subject in the [[accusative]]: “for him to go”, “that he goes”.

## greek-participle | Greek participles
lang: grc
aka: participle in Greek, attributive participle, adverbial participle, circumstantial participle, substantival participle
see: participle, genitive-absolute, greek-article, aspect
find: grc ^V-2?[PIFARL][A-Z]P-
short: A Greek verb used like an adjective, with case endings. It can describe someone (“the one who believes”) or set the scene for the main verb (“having said this, he went out”).

A [[participle]] has the [[tense]] and [[voice]] of a verb and the [[case]], [[number]] and [[gender]] of an [[adjective]]. Its endings include -ων, -ουσα, -ον (present active), -σας ([[aorist]] active) and -μενος ([[middle-or-passive|middle or passive]]).

With “the” in front, a participle names a person by what they do: ὁ πιστεύων, “the one who believes”, or “whoever believes”. English usually needs a “who” clause.

Without “the”, a participle usually tells you something about the main action: when, why or how it happened. εἰπὼν ταῦτα ἐξῆλθεν, “having said this, he went out”, or “when he had said this…”. As a rule, a [[present]] participle happens at the same time as the main verb (“while…”), and an [[aorist]] participle before it (“after…”).

Greek writers often string several participles before one main verb, where English would use “and” several times.

## genitive-absolute | Genitive absolute
lang: grc
aka: genitive absolute, absolute participle
see: greek-participle, genitive
short: A [[participle]] and its subject both in the [[genitive]], setting the scene: “while he was still speaking…”.

When a participle's own subject is not part of the main sentence, Greek puts both in the [[genitive]]: ἔτι αὐτοῦ λαλοῦντος, “while he was still speaking” ([MAT 12:46]). The phrase stands loose from the rest of the sentence (“absolute” means “loosed”), like a stage direction. Translate it with “while”, “when” or “after”.

## contract-verbs | Contract verbs
lang: grc
aka: contracted verbs, -έω verbs, -άω verbs, -όω verbs, contraction
see: greek-verb, stem, contracted
lessons: grc-5
short: Verbs whose [[stem]] ends in α, ε or ο, which merges with the vowel of the ending: ποιέω, “I do”, is written ποιῶ.

Some verb stems end in a vowel: ποιε-, ἀγαπα-, δικαιο-. When an ending starting with a vowel is added, the two vowels merge (contract): ποιε- + -ει becomes ποιεῖ, “he does”; ἀγαπα- + -ει becomes ἀγαπᾷ, “he loves”; δικαιο- + -εται becomes δικαιοῦται, “he is justified”.

Dictionaries list these verbs in their uncontracted form, which never appears in the text: ποιέω, ἀγαπάω, δικαιόω. That way you can see the stem's vowel. A circumflex accent (῀) on the ending is often a sign of contraction.

## eimi | The verb “to be”: εἰμί
lang: grc
aka: εἰμί, eimi, to be, I am, is, was, ἐστίν, ἦν
see: verb, verbless-clause, nominative
lessons: grc-9
short: The commonest and most irregular Greek verb: εἰμί, “I am”, ἐστίν, “he is”, ἦν, “he was”. It is followed by the [[nominative]], not the [[accusative]].

εἰμί has its own set of forms that must simply be learned:

Table: εἰμί, “I am”, now and in the past
| person | now | in the past |
| I | εἰμί | ἤμην |
| you (one) | εἶ | ἦς |
| he, she, it | ἐστίν | ἦν |
| we | ἐσμέν | ἦμεν |
| you (more than one) | ἐστέ | ἦτε |
| they | εἰσίν | ἦσαν |

What follows “is” describes the [[subject]], so it is in the [[nominative]] too: ὁ θεὸς ἀγάπη ἐστίν, “God is love”. Often ἐστίν is left out altogether, and English has to supply “is”: see [[verbless-clause]].

STEPBible's labels give εἰμί the voice “active”, as a convention: it has no passive. Its [[future]] has middle endings: ἔσομαι, “I will be”.

## principal-parts | Principal parts
lang: grc
aka: principal parts, main forms, verb stems, tense stems
see: second-aorist, greek-verb, dictionary-form
short: The handful of forms of a verb from which all its other forms are built. Learning them makes irregular verbs recognisable.

Every Greek verb has up to six main stems: the [[present]], [[future]], [[aorist]] active, [[perfect-greek|perfect]] active, perfect middle and aorist passive. All its other forms are built from one of these. For regular verbs they follow a pattern: πιστεύω, πιστεύσω, ἐπίστευσα, πεπίστευκα, πεπίστευμαι, ἐπιστεύθην. For irregular verbs they don't, which is why dictionaries list them: ὁράω, “I see”, has the aorist εἶδον and the future ὄψομαι.

A beginner doesn't need to learn whole lists. It helps to know that a strange-looking form may still be a common verb, and the app always shows the [[dictionary-form]].

## verbless-clause | Sentences without “is”
lang: both
aka: verbless clause, nominal sentence, missing verb, no verb, is supplied
see: eimi, subject, sentence-parts
short: Greek and especially Hebrew often leave out “is” or “are”, and English adds it: “blessed the man”, meaning “blessed *is* the man”.
lessons: he-10

English needs a verb in every sentence. Greek can leave out ἐστίν: μακάριοι οἱ πτωχοί, literally “blessed the poor”, is “blessed *are* the poor”. Hebrew does this all the time, because it has no common word for “is” in the present: יְהוָה רֹעִי, literally “the LORD my shepherd”, is “the LORD *is* my shepherd” ([PSA 23:1]).

That is why English Bibles add “is” or “are”, sometimes printing it in italics. In the app, a word that English adds is shown in square brackets in the word-by-word English.
