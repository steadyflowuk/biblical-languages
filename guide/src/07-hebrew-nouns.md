# hebrew-nouns | Hebrew words: roots, nouns and the little words

Hebrew builds its words on a root of consonants and joins many small words to the front or back of the main word. One Hebrew word can be a whole English phrase: וּבְבֵיתוֹ, “and in his house”. This section explains how to take such a word apart.

## root | Root
lang: he
aka: roots, three-letter root, triliteral, radicals, root letters
see: stems, hebrew-verb, dictionary-form, inflection
lessons: he-6
short: The consonants, usually three, that carry a family of related meanings: מ־ל־ך is behind מֶלֶךְ, “king”, and מָלַךְ, “he reigned”.

Most Hebrew words are built on a root of three consonants. The vowels and the letters added before, after or inside the root make different words from it, all with related meanings. From the root מ־ל־ך come מֶלֶךְ, “king”, מָלַךְ, “he reigned”, מַלְכָּה, “queen”, and מַמְלָכָה, “kingdom”.

Recognising the root is the main skill in reading Hebrew. Once you can find the three letters underneath, you can often guess the meaning of a word you have never seen, and you know where to look it up. Some roots have a weak letter that drops out or changes in some forms (often א, ה, ו, י or נ), which makes them harder to spot: see [[weak-verbs]].

## hebrew-noun | Hebrew nouns: gender, number and endings
lang: he
aka: Hebrew noun endings, plural endings, -im, -ot, masculine plural, feminine plural
see: noun, gender, number, dual, construct-state
lessons: he-6
find: he (^|/)Nc..a$
short: A Hebrew noun is masculine or feminine, and singular, plural or [[dual]]. Plurals usually end in -ִים (masculine) or -וֹת (feminine).

Most masculine nouns have no special ending in the singular, and add -ִים in the plural: סוּס, “horse”, סוּסִים, “horses”. Most feminine nouns end in -ָה or -ת, and their plural ends in -וֹת: מִצְוָה, “commandment”, מִצְוֹת, “commandments”.

There are exceptions, and the [[gender]] of the noun doesn't always match its plural ending: אָבוֹת, “fathers”, is masculine. As in Greek, the gender is a matter of grammar, but it decides the form of adjectives and verbs that go with the noun.

Hebrew nouns don't have [[case]] endings as Greek ones do. Instead, a noun's form changes when it is joined to the noun after it ([[construct-state]]), and when it takes an ending such as “my” or “his” ([[pronoun-suffix]]).

## dual | The dual: two of something
lang: he
aka: dual, pair, two, -ayim, du.
match: dual=two, a pair
see: number, hebrew-noun
find: he ^N..d n=2
short: A Hebrew noun form for two of something, ending -ַיִם: יָדַיִם, “two hands”. It is used mostly for things that come in pairs.

Hebrew has a special ending, -ַיִם, for two of something. It is used for pairs of body parts, such as עֵינַיִם, “eyes”, and רַגְלַיִם, “feet”, and for a few words of time and number: יוֹמַיִם, “two days”, שְׁנַיִם, “two”. Things that come in pairs keep the dual even when there are more than two: the seraphim each had שֵׁשׁ כְּנָפַיִם, “six wings” ([ISA 6:2]).

Two common words only look dual and are really ordinary words: שָׁמַיִם, “heaven”, and מַיִם, “water”.

## absolute-state | Absolute state
lang: he
aka: absolute, absolute state, abs., free form
match: absolute=the ordinary form of a noun, standing on its own
see: construct-state, hebrew-noun
short: The ordinary form of a Hebrew noun, standing on its own: דָּבָר, “word”. Its partner is the construct form, joined to the next word.

A Hebrew noun has two kinds of form. The absolute is the noun on its own, as in a dictionary: דָּבָר, “a word”, הַדָּבָר, “the word”. The [[construct-state]] is the form used when it is joined to the noun after it in an “of” phrase: דְּבַר, “word of”. STEPBible's labels say which one each noun is.

## construct-state | Construct state: “of” in Hebrew
lang: he
aka: construct, construct state, construct chain, cstr., const., genitive in Hebrew, of
match: construct=joined to the next word: “… of”
see: absolute-state, hebrew-noun, genitive, maqqef
find: he (^|/)Nc..c$
short: A Hebrew noun joined to the noun after it to mean “… of …”: דְּבַר יְהוָה, “the word of the LORD”. The first noun is in its construct form.

Hebrew has no word for “of”. It puts two nouns side by side, and the first one changes its form slightly, often shortening its vowels, to show it belongs to the second: דָּבָר, “word”, becomes דְּבַר in דְּבַר יְהוָה, “the word of the LORD”. This joined form is the construct. Two or more nouns joined this way are called a construct chain, and are often linked by a [[maqqef]].

Two rules help. The first noun never takes “the”. And the whole phrase counts as “the” if the last noun has “the” or is a name: בֵּית הַמֶּלֶךְ is “*the* house of the king”, though בֵּית has no article.

The construct is used for the same kinds of meaning as the Greek [[genitive]]: belonging, source, material, and description, as in הַר קָדְשִׁי, “my holy mountain”, literally “the mountain of my holiness”.

> GEN 15:1 דְבַר | דְבַר־יְהוָה, “the word of the LORD”: דְבַר is the construct of דָּבָר, joined to the next word by a maqqef

## hebrew-article | The Hebrew article: הַ
lang: he
aka: the, Hebrew article, ha-, definite article, הַ, article prefix
match: definite=with “the” built in
see: article, prefixes, dagesh, gutturals
lessons: he-7
unattested: לְהַמֶּלֶךְ
find: he (^|/)Td
short: Hebrew “the” is a prefix, הַ, joined to the front of a word, usually with a dot doubling the next letter: הַשָּׁמַיִם, “the heavens”.

The article is ה with a patah, and a [[dagesh]] that doubles the first letter of the word: מֶלֶךְ, “king”, הַמֶּלֶךְ, “the king”. Before the [[gutturals|throat letters]] and ר, which can't be doubled, the vowel changes instead. Before א, ע and ר it usually becomes הָ: הָאָרֶץ, “the earth”, הָעָם, “the people”. Before ה and ח it usually stays הַ, without the dot: הַחֹשֶׁךְ, “the darkness”. And before a ח with a qamets it becomes הֶ: הֶחָכָם, “the wise man”.

After the one-letter prepositions ב, כ and ל, the ה drops out and its vowel moves under the preposition: לַמֶּלֶךְ, “to the king”, instead of לְהַמֶּלֶךְ. STEPBible labels such a word “Preposition (definite)”.

Hebrew has no word for “a”. A noun without the article is indefinite, unless it is a name, has a “my”-type ending, or is joined to a definite noun ([[construct-state]]).

## prefixes | One-letter prefixes
lang: he
aka: prefix, prefixes, inseparable prefixes, inseparable prepositions, word parts, ו ב כ ל מ ה ש
see: conjunction-vav, hebrew-prepositions, hebrew-article, interrogative-particle, hebrew-relative
short: Several short Hebrew words are written as one letter joined to the front of the next word: וְ “and”, בְּ “in”, כְּ “like”, לְ “to”, מִ “from”, הַ “the”.

Hebrew writes some of its commonest words as a single letter with a vowel, joined to the word after them. A word in the Bible can carry two or three of them: וּבַיּוֹם, “and on the day”, is “and” + “on” + “the” + “day”.

- וְ, “and”: see [[conjunction-vav]];
- בְּ, “in”, “with”, “by”; כְּ, “like”, “as”; לְ, “to”, “for”: see [[hebrew-prepositions]];
- מִ, “from”, a short form of מִן;
- הַ, “the”: see [[hebrew-article]];
- הֲ, which turns a sentence into a question: see [[interrogative-particle]];
- שֶׁ, “who”, “which”: see [[hebrew-relative]].

When you tap a word in the Reader, “Word parts” shows each prefix and ending separately, with its meaning.

## conjunction-vav | וְ: “and”
lang: he
aka: vav, waw, and, וְ, וּ, conjunction vav, waw consecutive, vav consecutive, consecutive conjunction
match: consecutive conjunction=the “and” of a narrative verb (wayyiqtol)
see: conjunction, prefixes, wayyiqtol, weqatal
lessons: he-9
find: he ^C/ n=2
short: The letter ו joined to the front of a word means “and”, “but”, “so” or “then”. It is the commonest word in the Hebrew Bible.

The letter ו is called vav (grammars also spell it waw). As “and”, וְ is written as part of the next word. Its vowel changes with the sound after it: וּ before ב, מ or פ, or before a [[shewa]] (וּמֶלֶךְ, “and a king”); and in a few other places וַ or וָ.

Hebrew stories join almost every sentence with “and”, which is why older English Bibles start so many verses with “And”. Modern translations often use “then”, “so”, “but” or nothing at all.

A special form, וַ with a doubled letter after it, joins the [[wayyiqtol|narrative past]] form: וַיֹּאמֶר, “and he said”. STEPBible's labels call it the “consecutive conjunction”, and the app shows it as “and (narrative)”: see the entry on the wayyiqtol.

## hebrew-prepositions | Hebrew prepositions
lang: he
aka: Hebrew preposition, be-, ke-, le-, min, in, like, to, from, בְּ, כְּ, לְ, מִן
see: preposition, prefixes, pronoun-suffix, hebrew-article
lessons: he-8
find: he (^|/)R(/|$) n=2
short: Three common Hebrew prepositions are one letter joined to the next word: בְּ “in”, כְּ “like”, לְ “to”. Others are separate words: מִן “from”, עַל “on”, אֶל “to”.

The one-letter prepositions are written with a [[shewa]] (בְּ, כְּ, לְ), and their vowel changes with the word after them: לֵאלֹהִים, “to God”, בַּשָּׁמַיִם, “in the heavens”, where the vowel shows the article was there (see [[hebrew-article]]).

מִן, “from”, can be a separate word, or shortened to מִ with a doubled letter after it: מִמִּצְרַיִם, “from Egypt”. It also makes comparisons: “good from” means “better than” (see [[comparative]]).

Prepositions can take [[pronoun-suffix|pronoun endings]]: לוֹ, “to him”, בָּהּ, “in her”, מִמֶּנּוּ, “from him”. See [[pronoun-suffix]].

## pronoun-suffix | Pronoun endings: “my”, “his”, “them”
lang: he
aka: suffix, suffixes, pronominal suffix, pronoun suffix, possessive suffix, object suffix, ending
match: suffix=an ending for “my”, “his”, “them” and so on
see: pronoun, person, hebrew-prepositions, pgn
find: he /Sp n=2
short: A short ending that means “my”, “your”, “his”, “her”, “our” or “their” on a noun, and “me”, “him”, “them” on a verb or preposition: דְּבָרוֹ, “his word”.

Instead of separate words for “my” and “his”, Hebrew adds an ending to the word:

Table: the commonest endings, on סוּס, “horse”
| meaning | ending | example |
| my | -i | סוּסִי |
| your (a man) | -kha | סוּסְךָ |
| your (a woman) | -ekh | סוּסֵךְ |
| his | -o | סוּסוֹ |
| her | -ah | סוּסָהּ |
| our | -enu | סוּסֵנוּ |
| your (more than one) | -khem | סוּסְכֶם |
| their | -am | סוּסָם |

unattested: סוּסִי, סוּסְךָ, סוּסֵךְ, סוּסוֹ, סוּסָהּ, סוּסֵנוּ, סוּסְכֶם, סוּסָם

On a verb the ending is the object: שָׁמְרֵנִי, “keep me”. On a preposition it is what the preposition governs: לָהֶם, “to them”. With a plural noun the endings change slightly: דְּבָרָיו, “his words”.

In the Reader, these endings are listed under “Word parts” as a suffix, with a label such as “suffix 3ms”, which means “his” or “him”: see [[pgn]].

## directional-he | ה of direction: “towards”
lang: he
aka: directional he, he locale, he of direction, towards, -ah ending, directional suffix, directional
match: directional suffix=the ending ה, “towards”; directional=towards
see: pronoun-suffix, paragogic
find: he /Sd n=2
short: An unstressed -ָה at the end of a place word, meaning “towards” or “to”: אַרְצָה, “to the land”, הַשָּׁמַיְמָה, “towards the heavens”.

Hebrew can show direction with an ending instead of a preposition. It is the vowel and ה, -ָה, added to a word for a place or a direction: מִצְרַיְמָה, “to Egypt”, צָפוֹנָה, “northwards”. Unlike the feminine ending -ָה, it is never stressed. The app shows it as “towards (ending)”.

> GEN 15:5 הַשָּׁמַיְמָה | הַשָּׁמַיְמָה, “towards the heavens”: “look up to the sky”
> GEN 12:5 אַרְצָה | אַרְצָה כְּנַעַן, “to the land of Canaan”

## paragogic | Added endings: paragogic hé and nun
lang: he
aka: paragogic, paragogic he, paragogic nun, added ending, extra ending, hé
match: paragogic hé=an extra ה on the end of a word; paragogic nun=an extra נ on the end of a verb; paragogic=added on the end
see: cohortative, imperative, directional-he
find: he /S[hn] n=2
short: An extra ה or נ added to the end of a word, often for emphasis or the rhythm of the sentence, without changing the basic meaning.

“Paragogic” means “added on”. Hebrew sometimes adds -ָה to a verb, especially to “let me” or “let us” forms ([[cohortative]]) and to commands: שִׁמְעָה, “hear!”. And it sometimes adds -וּן to a plural verb: יִשְׁמְעוּן, “they will hear”. Grammar books describe these as giving some emphasis or a fuller sound. STEPBible's labels show them with “!”, and the app calls them an “added ending”.

## object-marker | אֵת: the object marker
lang: he
aka: et, object marker, object indicator, direct object marker, accusative marker, אֵת, אֶת
match: object indicator=the small word אֵת, marking the object
see: object, accusative, hebrew-prepositions
find: he (^|/)To n=2 key=^H0853
short: The small word אֵת (or אֶת־) marks the direct object of a verb when it is a particular, known person or thing. It has no English translation.

In [GEN 1:1], “God created *the heavens* and *the earth*”, the two objects each have אֵת in front of them: אֵת הַשָּׁמַיִם וְאֵת הָאָרֶץ. It shows which noun is the [[object]], since Hebrew word order is freer than English. It is used only when the object is definite: a name, a noun with “the”, or a noun with a “my”-type ending.

The app's English shows it as <obj.>, meaning “here is the object”, and leaves it untranslated. Don't confuse it with a different word, אֵת meaning “with”: the context decides.

## gentilic | People groups: gentilic nouns
lang: both
aka: gentilic, people group, nationality, -i ending, Hebrew (people), Israelite
match: gentilic=a word for a member of a people, such as “Israelite”; people=a people group
see: proper-noun, adjective
find: he ^N[gx] n=2
find: grc ^N-...-(L|P)?G$ n=2
short: A word for a member of a people or a place: עִבְרִי, “Hebrew”, יְהוּדִי, “Jew”, Γαλιλαῖος, “Galilean”.

These words are made from the name of a people or place, as English makes “Israelite” from “Israel”. In Hebrew they usually end in -ִי: מִצְרִי, “Egyptian”. STEPBible calls them gentilic (from Latin *gens*, “people”), and the app shows them as “(people group)”.

## hebrew-pronouns | Hebrew pronouns: אֲנִי, אַתָּה, הוּא
lang: he
aka: I, you, he, she, we, they, independent pronoun, personal pronoun in Hebrew, ani, anokhi, hu, hi
see: pronoun, pronoun-suffix, verbless-clause, person
find: he (^|/)Pp n=2
short: Separate Hebrew words for “I”, “you”, “he”, “she”, “we” and “they”, used mostly as the subject, often in sentences without a verb: אֲנִי יְהוָה, “I am the LORD”.
lessons: he-10

Table: the subject pronouns
| meaning | Hebrew |
| I | אֲנִי or אָנֹכִי |
| you (a man) | אַתָּה |
| you (a woman) | אַתְּ |
| he | הוּא |
| she | הִיא |
| we | אֲנַחְנוּ |
| you (men, more than one) | אַתֶּם |
| they (men) | הֵם or הֵמָּה |

A Hebrew verb already shows who does the action, so these words are used for emphasis, or in [[verbless-clause|sentences without a verb]], where they act like “is”: אֲנִי יְהוָה, “I [am] the LORD”. For “me”, “my”, “him” and “his”, Hebrew uses [[pronoun-suffix|endings]] instead.

## hebrew-relative | אֲשֶׁר: “who”, “which”, “that”
lang: he
aka: asher, relative particle, who, which, that, אֲשֶׁר, שֶׁ
match: relative particle=the word אֲשֶׁר, “who”, “which”, “that”; relative=the word אֲשֶׁר, “who”, “which”, “that”
see: relative, prefixes, sentence-parts
find: he (^|/)Tr n=2
short: The Hebrew word that joins a clause to a noun: “the land *which* I will show you”. It never changes its form.

Unlike Greek [[relative|ὅς]], אֲשֶׁר has no endings. Hebrew often adds a pronoun later in the clause to show how it fits: “the land which you are lying on it” means “the land on which you are lying”. English leaves that extra word out.

A short form, שֶׁ, is joined to the next word, mostly in poetry and later books, such as Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs.

## interrogative-particle | Question markers
lang: both
aka: question, interrogative particle, hă-, הֲ, question marker
match: interrogative particle=a word that turns a sentence into a question
see: interrogative, prefixes, negative
find: he (^|/)Ti n=2
short: A word that turns a sentence into a question. In Hebrew it is the prefix הֲ: הֲשֹׁמֵר אָחִי אָנֹכִי, “Am I my brother's keeper?”.

Hebrew writes no question mark. A question is shown by a question word such as מִי, “who?”, or by the prefix הֲ on the first word of a yes-or-no question. Don't confuse הֲ with the article הַ: the vowel is usually different, and the question הֲ doesn't double the next letter. Before a [[shewa]] or a [[gutturals|throat letter]] it is written הַ, and then only the missing dot tells them apart.

Greek has question particles too, and STEPBible labels them the same way, but a Greek question is usually shown by the question mark ; at the end (see [[greek-punctuation]]).

## demonstrative-particle | זֶה, זֹאת, אֵלֶּה: “this”, “these”
lang: he
aka: zeh, zot, elleh, this, these, demonstrative particle, Hebrew demonstrative
match: demonstrative particle=“this” or “these”: a pointing word
see: demonstrative, hebrew-article, hinneh
find: he (^|/)Tm n=3
short: The Hebrew words for “this” and “these”: זֶה (masculine), זֹאת (feminine) and אֵלֶּה (plural). STEPBible labels them “demonstrative particle”.

They point to someone or something near, or just mentioned. After a noun with “the”, they take “the” too, and mean “this”: הַיּוֹם הַזֶּה, “this day”, literally “the day, the this”. On their own they mean “this is” or “these are”: אֵלֶּה הַדְּבָרִים, “these are the words”.

For “that” and “those”, Hebrew uses the words for “he”, “she” and “they”: הַיּוֹם הַהוּא, “that day”. The app's short label for these words is “this/that”.

## hinneh | הִנֵּה: “look!”
lang: he
aka: hinneh, behold, look, here I am, הִנֵּה, הֵן
see: interjection, demonstrative-particle
find: he key=^H2009 n=3
short: הִנֵּה, “look!”, “here is”, “behold”, draws attention to what follows. STEPBible labels it an interjection.

Older English Bibles translate it “behold”. Modern ones use “look”, “see”, “here is”, or show its sense with a word such as “suddenly” or “now”. It can take a pronoun ending: הִנֵּנִי, “here I am”, is Abraham's and Samuel's answer when God calls ([GEN 22:1], [1SA 3:4]), and Isaiah's answer is הִנְנִי ([ISA 6:8]).

## conditional-particle | כִּי and אִם: “if”, “for”, “that”
lang: he
aka: ki, im, if, for, because, that, כִּי, אִם, conditional particle
match: conditional particle=a word such as כִּי or אִם: “if”, “for”, “that”
see: conjunction, sentence-parts
find: he (^|/)Tc n=2
short: Two very common Hebrew words. כִּי means “for”, “because”, “that” or “when”; אִם means “if”. STEPBible labels both “conditional particle”.

כִּי is one of the most used words in the Hebrew Bible, and its meaning depends on its place: “for” or “because” giving a reason, “that” after verbs of seeing and knowing, “when”, or “surely”. אִם means “if”, or “whether”, and in oaths it can mean “surely not”. כִּי אִם together often means “but rather” or “except”. STEPBible gives the same label to a few other joining words, such as לְמַעַן, “so that”, and פֶּן, “so that not”.

The app shows both as “conj. (if/for)” under the word.
