# greek-writing | Greek letters, marks and punctuation

Printed Greek has small marks above and beside its letters, and a few punctuation signs of its own. Most were added long after the New Testament was written, to help readers. This section explains each one. The Letters tab covers the alphabet itself, letter by letter, with sounds.

## greek-alphabet | The Greek alphabet
lang: grc
aka: alphabet, Greek letters, alpha beta, letters
see: breathing, accents, final-sigma, erasmian, restored-koine
lessons: grc-1, grc-2
short: Greek has twenty-four letters, seven of them vowels. English took many of its letters from it, through Latin.

The word “alphabet” comes from the first two Greek letters, alpha and beta. Many Greek letters look and sound like ours: α a, β b, κ k, τ t. Some look like ours but stand for other sounds: η is a long e, ρ is r, ν is n, χ is kh. And a few are new shapes: γ, δ, θ, λ, ξ, π, σ, φ, ψ, ω.

The [[#/alphabet/grc|Letters tab]] shows every letter with its name, its sound in both pronunciations ([[erasmian]] and [[restored-koine]]), and examples you can hear.

## greek-vowels | Greek vowels: long, short and pairs
lang: grc
aka: vowels, Greek vowels, long vowels, short vowels, diphthong, diphthongs, vowel pairs, αι, ει, οι, ου, αυ, ευ
see: greek-alphabet, diaeresis, iota-subscript, erasmian, restored-koine
lessons: grc-2
short: Greek has seven vowels: α ε η ι ο υ ω. η and ω are always long, ε and ο always short, and α, ι and υ can be either. Two vowels together often make one sound, such as ου, oo.

The seven vowels come in two kinds. ε and η are a short and a long e; ο and ω a short and a long o. α, ι and υ can be long or short, and the spelling doesn't show which. Length matters for the [[accents]] and for the shape of endings, though the app's voices don't hold long vowels any longer than short ones.

Some pairs of vowels are said as one sound, a diphthong (“two sounds”). In the app's [[erasmian|Erasmian]] pronunciation:

Table: the vowel pairs
| pair | said | example |
| αι | ai, as in “aisle” | καί, kai, “and” |
| ει | ay, as in “eight” | εἰμί, ay-MEE, “I am” |
| οι | oi, as in “oil” | οἶκος, OI-kos, “house” |
| ου | oo, as in “food” | οὐρανός, oo-ra-NOS, “heaven” |
| αυ | ow, as in “how” | αὐτός, ow-TOS, “he” |
| ευ | eu, as in “feud” | εὐαγγέλιον, eu-ang-GE-lee-on, “good news” |
| υι | wee | υἱός, hwee-OS, “son” |

In [[restored-koine|Restored Koine]] most of these had become single vowels by the first century: the [[letters-tab|Letters tab]] shows both side by side. Two dots over the second vowel ([[diaeresis]]) mean the pair is said as two sounds after all, and a small ι under a long vowel ([[iota-subscript]]) is not said at all.

## breathing | Breathing marks
lang: grc
aka: breathing, rough breathing, smooth breathing, spiritus, h sound, ῾, ᾿
see: accents, greek-alphabet, erasmian
lessons: grc-3
short: A small mark like an apostrophe over a word's first vowel. A rough breathing (῾) adds an h sound; a smooth breathing (᾿) adds nothing.

Almost every Greek word that begins with a vowel has a breathing mark on it. The rough breathing, curved like an opening quotation mark, means the word starts with an h: ὁ is “ho”, ἁμαρτία is “hamartia”. The smooth breathing, curved the other way, means no h: ἐν is “en”.

A few rules: a word starting with ρ nearly always has a rough breathing (ῥῆμα), which isn't said, and a word starting with υ always does (υἱός). A few Aramaic words written in Greek letters, such as Αββα, have no breathing at all. When a word starts with a pair of vowels, the mark sits on the second: αὐτός, εἰς. On a capital letter it stands in front: Ἰησοῦς.

In the [[erasmian|Erasmian]] pronunciation the h is said. In [[restored-koine]] it is not, because by the first century the h sound had dropped out of everyday Greek, and the voice can't say it either way.

## accents | Accents
lang: grc
aka: accent, acute, grave, circumflex, accent marks, ´, `, ῀, tonos
see: breathing, stress, enclitic
lessons: grc-3
short: Small marks over a vowel (´ ` ῀) that show which syllable to stress. They were added by later scholars, and today all three simply mean “stress here”.

Greek has three accents. The acute (´) rises to the right, the grave (`) falls to the right, and the circumflex (῀) is a small wave. In ancient Greek they showed a rise and fall of pitch, a little like a tune. By New Testament times Greek was stressed like English, and in both of the app's pronunciations each accent just marks the stressed syllable: λόγος is LO-gos, ἀγάπη is a-GA-pay.

An acute on the last syllable of a word turns into a grave when another word follows without a pause: τόν becomes τὸν in τὸν λόγον. It is the same word.

Accents sometimes tell apart words that are otherwise spelt alike: εἰ, “if”, and εἶ, “you are”; ἡ, “the”, ἥ, “who”, and ἤ, “or”.

The earliest manuscripts of the New Testament have no accents. Scholars in Alexandria had invented them earlier, but scribes only began to write them regularly centuries later. The accents in printed Bibles come from that later tradition.

## enclitic | Words with no accent of their own
lang: grc
aka: enclitic, proclitic, enclitics, proclitics, leaning word
see: accents, indefinite
short: A few short words lean on their neighbour and have no accent of their own: μου, “my”, and τις, “someone”, lean back; ὁ, ἐν and οὐ lean forward.

Some short Greek words are said together with the word next to them, as if they were part of it. Enclitics, such as μου, σου, τις and often ἐστιν, lean on the word before. They usually lose their accent, and sometimes give an extra one to the word they lean on: ὁ λόγος μου, “my word”; ἄνθρωπός τις, “a certain man”. Proclitics, such as ὁ, ἡ, ἐν, εἰς, ἐκ and οὐ, lean on the word after, and have no accent.

That is why the app's respellings show these words without a stressed syllable, and why ἄνθρωπός, before τις, carries two accents.

## iota-subscript | Iota subscript
lang: grc
aka: iota subscript, subscript, silent iota, ᾳ, ῃ, ῳ
see: dative, accents
short: A small ι written under α, η or ω (ᾳ ῃ ῳ). It is not said today, but it often marks the [[dative]] case.

Once these were long α, η and ω followed by a real ι, but the ι stopped being said, and scribes later wrote it as a tiny letter underneath. It doesn't change the pronunciation, but it matters for reading, because it often marks the [[dative]] case: τῷ λόγῳ, “to the word”; τῇ ἡμέρᾳ, “on the day”.

## diaeresis | Diaeresis: two dots
lang: grc
aka: diaeresis, trema, two dots, ¨, dieresis
see: breathing, greek-alphabet
short: Two dots over a vowel (ϊ ϋ) mean it is said on its own, not as part of a pair: Ἠσαΐας, “Isaiah”, is said ay-sa-EE-as.

Pairs of vowels such as αι and ευ are usually said as one sound. When they are not, the second vowel has two dots: Ἠσαΐας is four syllables, not three. You will see this mostly in names taken from Hebrew, such as Ἠσαΐας and Μωϋσῆς, “Moses”.

## elision | Elision: a dropped vowel
lang: grc
aka: elision, elided, apostrophe, dropped vowel, ’, ʼ
see: crasis, movable-nu
short: A short vowel at the end of a word is often dropped before a word starting with a vowel, and an apostrophe marks the place: δι’ αὐτοῦ, “through him”, for διά αὐτοῦ.

Greek avoids two vowels meeting across words, so it often drops the first one: ἀπ’ ἐμοῦ for ἀπό ἐμοῦ, “from me”; δι’ αὐτοῦ for διὰ αὐτοῦ. If the next word has a rough [[breathing|breathing]], the consonant before the apostrophe may change too: ἀφ’ ἑαυτοῦ, “from himself”, for ἀπὸ ἑαυτοῦ.

The SBL Greek New Testament writes the apostrophe as ʼ. The meaning is exactly that of the full word, and the app's dictionary lists it under the full form.

## crasis | Crasis: two words merged into one
lang: grc
aka: crasis, contraction of words, κἀγώ, merged words
see: elision, contracted
short: Two words run together into one, with a mark showing the join: κἀγώ is καί + ἐγώ, “and I”.

Crasis (Greek for “mixing”) merges two words into one. In the New Testament it is mostly καί with a word starting with a vowel: κἀγώ, “and I”, κἀκεῖνος, “and that one”, κἄν, “even if”. The mark over the join looks like a smooth [[breathing|breathing]], but it marks the merge. The app shows these words as joined from two, with both parts' meanings.

## movable-nu | Movable ν
lang: grc
aka: movable nu, nu movable, ephelkystic nu, extra ν
unattested: λέγουσι
see: elision
short: Some word endings can take an extra ν before a vowel or at a pause: ἐστί or ἐστίν, “is”. The word is the same either way.

To avoid two vowels meeting, Greek adds ν to certain endings that end in a vowel, especially -σι, -ε and ἐστί: λέγουσι or λέγουσιν, “they say”. Writers and scribes didn't always follow the rule, and the SBL text often has the ν even before a consonant. It never changes the meaning.

## final-sigma | Final sigma: σ and ς
lang: grc
aka: final sigma, sigma, σ, ς, lunate sigma
see: greek-alphabet
short: The letter s has two shapes: σ inside a word and ς at the end. They are the same letter.

λόγος has both: σ is never written at the end of a word, and ς never inside one. The capital is Σ. Ancient manuscripts mostly wrote a third shape, like a C, everywhere, and some printed editions copy it. The [[words-tab|Words tab]] treats all of them as the same letter when you search.

## greek-punctuation | Greek punctuation
lang: grc
aka: punctuation, question mark, raised dot, ano teleia, ;, ·, full stop, comma
see: capitals-greek, double-brackets
short: Greek uses . and , as English does, a raised dot · for a pause like a colon or semicolon, and ; for a question mark.

The Greek question mark looks like an English semicolon: τίς ἐστιν; “who is he?”. The raised dot · marks a longer pause than a comma: often it comes just before someone's words, where English would put a colon.

The first manuscripts had almost no punctuation, and often no spaces between words. Modern editors add it, and editions sometimes punctuate differently. Occasionally that changes how a verse is read: [JHN 1:3] and [JHN 1:4] can be divided in two ways, and translations differ.

## capitals-greek | Capital letters in the Greek text
lang: grc
aka: capitals, capital letter, upper case, capitalisation
see: greek-punctuation, proper-noun
short: The Greek text uses capitals for names, for the start of a paragraph and for the start of someone's words, but not for every sentence.

English starts each sentence with a capital. The SBL Greek New Testament doesn't: a new sentence in the middle of a paragraph starts with a small letter. Capitals mark names (Ἰησοῦς, Γαλιλαία), the first word of a paragraph, and the first word of a quotation: Ἐγώ εἰμι, “I am”, when someone starts speaking.

## double-brackets | Double brackets ⟦ ⟧
lang: grc
aka: double brackets, ⟦, ⟧, brackets, later addition, longer ending of Mark, woman caught in adultery, pericope
see: manuscripts, greek-text
short: Double square brackets mark passages that most scholars think were added to the text after it was first written, such as Mark 16:9–20 and John 7:53–8:11.

Some passages are missing from the oldest and best manuscripts but are found in many later ones. The SBL Greek New Testament prints the two longest of these, the longer ending of Mark ([MRK 16:9]) and the story of the woman caught in adultery ([JHN 7:53]), between ⟦ and ⟧, and the short ending of Mark after [MRK 16:8] too. The Reader puts a note wherever they appear.

The brackets don't say the passages are untrue or should be ignored. They say that the editors think they were not part of the book as first written. Many Bibles print them with a similar note.

## paragraph-marks | Paragraphs in the Greek text
lang: grc
aka: paragraph, paragraphs, ¶, running text
see: greek-punctuation, petuha-setuma
short: The Greek text is divided into paragraphs by its editors, and the Reader's running-text layout shows them.

Like punctuation, paragraphs are the editors' choice, not the writers'. The SBL Greek New Testament marks where each paragraph begins. In the Reader's running-text layout these become the paragraphs on the page. In the word-by-word layout each verse stands on its own, so paragraphs don't show. The Hebrew text has its own paragraph marks: see [[petuha-setuma]].

## greek-numerals | Letters used as numbers
lang: grc
aka: Greek numerals, numbers written as letters, ιδ᾽, β᾽, numerals in the Septuagint
see: septuagint, numbers-words, greek-alphabet
short: In the [[septuagint]] some numbers are written with letters and a mark: β᾽ is two, ι᾽ ten, ιδ᾽ fourteen. The app shows each one's value and reads it as a number.

Greeks used their letters as numbers: α is one, β two, γ three and so on, ι ten, κ twenty, ρ a hundred. A small mark after the letters shows that they are a number, not a word: ιδ᾽ is ten plus four, fourteen. The New Testament writes numbers out as words, but Swete's Septuagint uses these figures in many lists. When you tap one, the app gives its value, and the Listen button says the number as a Greek word in the right form.
unattested: β᾽, ι᾽, ιδ᾽
