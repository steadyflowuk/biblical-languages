# start | Start here

This guide explains every word, sign and idea you meet in the app, starting from nothing. You don't need to have learned another language before. Each entry starts with a one-line answer, then explains more slowly, with real examples from the Bible that you can hear and open in the Reader.

Tap any underlined word to read its own entry. Tap Greek or Hebrew to hear it.

## using-the-guide | Using this guide
aka: help, how to use the guide, glossary, explanations
short: Search for a word, browse by section, or tap “What do these words mean?” under any grammar in the app.

There are three ways in. You can type a word in the search box at the top of the Guide tab: an English word such as “aorist”, an abbreviation such as “impf.” or “3ms”, or a grammar code such as V-2AAI-3S. You can browse the sections, which go from the simplest ideas to the hardest. Or you can start from the Bible: when you tap a word in the Reader, on Today or in a lesson, its grammar has a link, “What do these words mean?”, which explains each part.

Entries link to each other. If an explanation uses a word you don't know yet, that word is underlined: tap it, read the short answer, and come back with the back button.

Most entries end with examples from the Bible. The word the entry is about is highlighted. Tap Listen to hear it, or “Open in the Reader” to see it in its chapter.

If you are just starting, read the section [[grammar|Grammar words everyone needs]] first. It explains words such as [[noun]], [[verb]] and [[subject]] using English, before any Greek or Hebrew.

## koine-greek | Koine Greek
lang: grc
aka: biblical Greek, New Testament Greek, common Greek, Greek
see: greek-text, septuagint, erasmian, restored-koine
short: The everyday Greek spoken around the eastern Mediterranean in the time of Jesus, and the language of the New Testament.
lessons: grc-0

Greek has been written for nearly three thousand years. The kind used in the New Testament is called Koine, a Greek word meaning “common” or “shared”. After the conquests of Alexander the Great, Greek became the shared language of trade, government and travel from Egypt to the Middle East. Many people spoke it as a second language.

The writers of the New Testament wrote in this common Greek, not in the polished Greek of the older classical writers. That is good news for a learner: its sentences are usually direct, and a small number of words make up most of the text.

Koine is written with the same alphabet as Greek today, but it is not the same as Modern Greek. Many words are spelt the same, while their sounds, some endings and many meanings have changed. Greek Bibles also add [[accents]] and [[breathing|breathing marks]] that the first writers didn't use.

The Old Testament was also translated into Koine before the time of Jesus: see [[septuagint]].

## biblical-hebrew | Biblical Hebrew
lang: he
aka: Hebrew, classical Hebrew, Old Testament Hebrew
match: Hebrew=Hebrew, not Aramaic
see: hebrew-text, aramaic-language, vowel-points, root
short: The language in which almost all of the Old Testament was written.
lessons: he-0

Hebrew belongs to the same family of languages as Aramaic and Arabic. It was spoken in ancient Israel, and the books of the Old Testament were written in it over many centuries. A few chapters are in Aramaic instead: see [[aramaic-language]].

Hebrew is written from right to left. Its alphabet has only consonants: the vowels were added later as small dots and dashes around the letters ([[vowel-points]]). Most words are built on a [[root]] of three consonants, and a great deal of the meaning comes from the vowels and the small additions placed around that root.

Modern Hebrew, spoken in Israel today, grew out of Biblical Hebrew. The letters and many words are the same, but the grammar and vocabulary have changed. The app's pronunciation is based on how Hebrew is said today, the way most colleges teach it: see [[hebrew-pronunciation]].

## aramaic-language | Biblical Aramaic
lang: arc
aka: Aramaic, Chaldee
see: aramaic-stems, biblical-hebrew
match: Aramaic=Aramaic, not Hebrew
short: A sister language of Hebrew, used in a few chapters of Daniel and Ezra and in some words of Jesus.

Aramaic was the shared language of the Persian empire, and many Jews spoke it after the exile. It uses the same letters and vowel signs as Hebrew, and many words are similar, so the Reader shows it in the same way and labels it “Aram.”.

In the Old Testament, Aramaic is used in [DAN 2:4|Daniel 2:4] to the end of chapter seven, in parts of Ezra (from [EZR 4:8|Ezra 4:8], and in chapter seven), in one verse of Jeremiah ([JER 10:11]) and in two words of Genesis ([GEN 31:47]).

The New Testament is in Greek, but it keeps a few Aramaic words just as Jesus said them, written in Greek letters: Ταλιθα κουμ in [MRK 5:41], and Αββα, “Father”, in [MRK 14:36]. The [[grammar-labels]] call these “transcribed from Aramaic”. See [[aramaic-stems]] for how Aramaic verbs are labelled.

## septuagint | The Septuagint
lang: grc
aka: LXX, Greek Old Testament, Swete
see: koine-greek, septuagint-lookup, hebrew-text
short: The Greek translation of the Old Testament, made by Jewish translators in the centuries before Jesus.

The Septuagint was translated from Hebrew into [[koine-greek]], beginning in Alexandria in Egypt with the five books of Moses. Its name comes from the Latin for “seventy”, after an old story about seventy-two translators, which is why it is shortened to LXX.

It matters for two reasons. The New Testament writers often quote it, so its wording is behind many New Testament quotations. And it shows how Jews of that time understood Hebrew words in Greek, which helps with the meaning of both languages.

The Septuagint doesn't always match the Hebrew text verse for verse: some books are arranged differently, some are longer or shorter, and it includes books that are not in the Hebrew Bible. The app uses the edition by H. B. Swete, printed in Cambridge. In the Reader you can show it under the Hebrew, or read it on its own from the book list. Its words are not tagged with grammar, so a tapped word is looked up by its spelling: see [[septuagint-lookup]].

## greek-text | The Greek New Testament in the app
lang: grc
aka: SBLGNT, SBL Greek New Testament, Greek text
see: manuscripts, koine-greek, double-brackets
short: The app's New Testament is the SBL Greek New Testament, an edition made by comparing the manuscripts.
lessons: grc-0

No original letter of Paul or first copy of a Gospel survives. What we have are thousands of handwritten copies (manuscripts) made over many centuries, and they differ in small ways. Scholars compare them to work out the most likely original wording: see [[manuscripts]].

The app uses the SBL Greek New Testament, edited by Michael Holmes and published by the Society of Biblical Literature. It is free to share, which is why the app can use it. Its wording is very close to the other editions used in colleges.

Every word is tagged with its [[dictionary-form]], its meaning in that verse and its grammar, using data from STEPBible (Tyndale House, Cambridge). The [[grammar-labels]] are explained in their own entry.

## hebrew-text | The Hebrew Bible in the app
lang: he
aka: Leningrad Codex, Masoretic Text, Hebrew text, WLC, TAHOT
see: masoretes, ketiv-qere, vowel-points, cantillation
short: The app's Old Testament is the Leningrad Codex, the oldest complete handwritten copy of the Hebrew Bible.
lessons: he-0

The Leningrad Codex was copied around the year 1008 and is now kept in St Petersburg (once called Leningrad). It is a careful copy of the text handed down by the [[masoretes]], the scribes who added the vowels and [[cantillation|accent marks]].

Because it is complete and carefully made, almost every printed Hebrew Bible today is based on it. The app uses STEPBible's edition, in which every word is split into its parts and tagged with its meaning and grammar.

Sometimes a word is written one way but read another way, and the Masoretes noted both: see [[ketiv-qere]]. A few words missing from this copy are supplied from other manuscripts: see [[supplied-words]].

## masoretes | The Masoretes
lang: he
aka: Masoretic, scribes, Tiberian
see: vowel-points, cantillation, ketiv-qere, hebrew-text
short: The Jewish scribes who, over several centuries, added vowels, accents and notes to the Hebrew text so it would be read exactly as handed down.

For most of its history the Hebrew Bible was written with consonants only. Readers knew the vowels from memory and from hearing the text read aloud. Between about the sixth and tenth centuries, scholars in Tiberias, by the Sea of Galilee, invented a system of marks to record the reading they had received, without changing a single letter.

They added three kinds of marks: [[vowel-points]], which show the vowels; [[cantillation|accent]] marks, which show the stress, the pauses and how the verse was chanted; and notes in the margins counting words and letters and recording places where a word is read differently from how it is written ([[ketiv-qere]]).

## manuscripts | Manuscripts and different readings
aka: textual criticism, variants, readings, apparatus, textual variant
see: greek-text, hebrew-text, double-brackets
short: Handwritten copies of the Bible differ in small ways; scholars compare them to find the most likely original wording.

Before printing, every copy of a book was written out by hand. Copyists sometimes made slips, like a word written twice or a line skipped, and sometimes tidied the wording. So the thousands of surviving manuscripts differ here and there. Most differences are tiny, such as spelling or word order, and very few change the meaning of a sentence.

Printed Greek and Hebrew Bibles choose one reading for the main text and often list other readings at the foot of the page (the apparatus). The app shows the main text only. Where a whole passage is doubtful, as in [MRK 16:9] or [JHN 7:53], the Greek text puts it in [[double-brackets]] and the Reader explains why.

## translations | The English translations
aka: BSB, Berean Standard Bible, ESV, English Standard Version, English
see: gloss, reader-layouts
app: reader-translations
short: The Reader shows the Berean Standard Bible under each verse, and the ESV too if you add your own free key.

The Berean Standard Bible (BSB) is a modern translation that is free for anyone to use, so the app can include all of it. It is shown under each verse and in the word details.

The English Standard Version (ESV) belongs to its publisher, Crossway. They give free keys to people for their own use: you can get one and enter it in Settings, and the ESV then shows in the Reader alongside the BSB. Only a small part is ever saved on your phone, as the publisher asks.

A translation turns whole sentences into natural English, so the English words don't line up one for one with the Greek or Hebrew. The meaning shown under each original word is closer to the word itself: see [[gloss]].

## verse-numbers | Chapters and verse numbers
aka: verse numbering, chapter and verse, versification, Heb, Hebrew numbering
see: psalm-titles, translations
short: Chapters and verses were added long after the Bible was written, and editions sometimes number them differently.

The writers didn't divide their books into chapters and verses. Chapters were added in the Middle Ages, and verse numbers later still, for finding places. Different traditions didn't always divide the text in the same place.

The app uses English numbering everywhere so the original and the translation line up. Where Hebrew Bibles number a verse differently, the Reader shows a small “Heb” number under it. This happens most in the Psalms, where Hebrew Bibles count a psalm's title as verse one: see [[psalm-titles]].

The Greek New Testament and the English also differ in a few places. Some verses printed in older English Bibles are not in the best manuscripts, so they are missing from both the Greek and the BSB, and the numbering simply skips them: after [MAT 17:20|Matthew 17:20] comes verse twenty-two. The Reader puts a note wherever the two don't match.

## strongs | Strong's numbers
aka: Strong's, Strong number, concordance number, G number, H number
see: dictionary-form, how-common
short: A number for each Hebrew and Greek dictionary word, so you can look a word up without knowing the language.

James Strong published a huge index of the English Bible in 1890, and gave each Hebrew and Greek dictionary word a number. Hebrew words have numbers starting H, Greek words numbers starting G: H430 is אֱלֹהִים, God, and G3056 is λόγος, word. Many books and websites still use them.

The app uses STEPBible's improved version. Where one of Strong's numbers covers two different words, or different meanings of one word, STEPBible adds a letter to tell them apart. You can type a Strong's number into the Words tab to find its entry.

## dictionary-form | Dictionary form
aka: lemma, lexical form, headword, root form, lexicon form
see: inflection, strongs, gloss
short: The form of a word you look up in a dictionary, such as “run” for “ran” and “running”.
lessons: grc-0, he-0

Words change their shape in a sentence. In English, “ran”, “runs” and “running” are all forms of “run”. A dictionary lists only “run”. That basic form is called the dictionary form (or lemma).

Greek and Hebrew change shape much more than English. One Greek verb can appear in more than a hundred different forms. So in the app, every word in the Bible shows both what it is in that verse and its dictionary form. Greek verbs are listed by their “I” form: λύω, “I loose”. Greek nouns are listed in the [[nominative]] singular: λόγος. Hebrew verbs are listed by the simplest form, the [[qal]] [[perfect-hebrew|qatal]] “he did”: בָּרָא, “he created”.

When you tap a word, you see the form in the verse first, then “Dictionary form”. In the Words tab you can search for either.

## gloss | Gloss and meaning
aka: meaning, definition, translation of a word, in this verse, dictionary meaning
see: dictionary-form, translations
short: A gloss is a short meaning for a word: one or two English words, not a full definition.

When you tap a word, the app shows three kinds of meaning. “In this verse” is the English for this word in this place, from STEPBible's word-by-word translation. “Dictionary meaning” is the usual meaning of the [[dictionary-form]]. The full entry in the Words tab gives a longer definition from an older dictionary.

Words in square brackets, such as [the], are added in English to make sense but have no word of their own in Greek or Hebrew. Words in angle brackets, such as <the>, are in the original but are not translated, because English doesn't need them.

## transliteration | Transliteration
aka: transliterated, translit, pronunciation, Latin letters, romanisation
see: respelling, erasmian, hebrew-pronunciation
short: Writing a Greek or Hebrew word in our alphabet, letter by letter, so you can see what it is made of.

A transliteration replaces each Greek or Hebrew letter with a letter or two of ours: λόγος becomes logos. It is a help for reading, not an exact guide to sound. The app's transliterations come from STEPBible. In them, long Greek vowels carry a line (ē for η, ō for ω), and Hebrew syllables are divided with dots, with the stressed syllable in capitals.

The app also shows a simpler [[respelling|respelling]] for saying a word aloud, such as LO-gos.

## respelling | Saying a word: the respelling
aka: pronunciation guide, say, how to say it, stress, capitals
see: transliteration, erasmian, hebrew-pronunciation, stress
short: The app writes how a word sounds in plain English letters, with the stressed syllable in capitals: LO-gos.

Next to many words you will see a respelling such as LO-gos or e-lo-HEEM. The hyphens split the word into syllables, and the syllable in capitals is the one to stress, the way we stress the first syllable of “TA-ble”.

For Greek the respelling follows the [[erasmian|Erasmian]] pronunciation. For Hebrew it follows the [[hebrew-pronunciation|pronunciation taught in most colleges]]. A few combinations need a word of explanation: kh is the sound at the end of Scottish “loch”, ch in Hebrew respellings is the same throat sound, and ü in Greek is said like French “tu”.

## erasmian | Erasmian pronunciation
lang: grc
aka: Erasmus, college pronunciation, academic Greek pronunciation
see: restored-koine, respelling, koine-greek
short: The way most colleges pronounce New Testament Greek, keeping almost every letter's sound distinct.
lessons: grc-1, grc-2

Nobody can hear first-century Greek, so learners need an agreed way of saying it. The Erasmian pronunciation is named after the scholar Erasmus, who wrote about how ancient Greek may have sounded. It gives nearly every letter and pair of letters its own sound. η and ει are both “ay”, ω is “oh”, and υ is like French “u”.

Its great advantage is spelling: if you hear a word, you can usually tell how it is written. It is not how Greek sounded in the time of Jesus, and it is not how Greek sounds today. The app uses it by default, and the lessons write pronunciations this way. You can switch to [[restored-koine]] in Settings.

## restored-koine | Restored Koine pronunciation
lang: grc
aka: Koine pronunciation, first-century pronunciation, Buth, historical pronunciation
see: erasmian, koine-greek
short: An attempt to say New Testament Greek as it was said in the first century, closer to Modern Greek.

Scholars, especially Randall Buth, have worked out from spelling mistakes in ancient papyri, poetry and later Greek how Koine probably sounded in the first century. By then several vowels and pairs had merged: ει and ι were both “ee”, αι sounded like ε, and υ and οι both sounded like French “u”. β was already v, and δ was like the “th” in “this”.

Choose it in Settings if you want to hear the Greek closer to how the apostles spoke. The respellings in the app stay [[erasmian|Erasmian]], so the letters stay clear. Because the app's Greek voice is a modern one, a few first-century sounds can only be imitated.

## hebrew-pronunciation | Hebrew pronunciation in the app
lang: he
aka: Israeli pronunciation, academic pronunciation, Sephardi, how Hebrew is said
see: masoretes, shewa, qamets-hatuf, stress
short: The app says Hebrew the way most colleges teach it: based on Israeli Hebrew, with every written vowel said.
lessons: he-1, he-3

There is no single way of saying [[biblical-hebrew]]. The app follows the pronunciation taught in most colleges, which is based on how Hebrew is spoken in Israel today. For example, ח is a throat sound like the end of Scottish “loch”, and the letters א and ע are silent.

Two rules keep it closer to the written text than everyday Israeli speech: a [[shewa]] at the start of a syllable is said as a quick e (בְּרִית is be-REET, not brit), and the [[qamets-hatuf]] is said o. God's name is read Adonai, as Jewish readers say it: see [[divine-name]].

The [[masoretes]] who wrote the vowels heard more differences than we make today. The Letters tab explains how it sounded then.

## grammar-labels | Grammar labels
aka: grammar, parsing, tags, morphology, grammar code, parse, label
see: grammar, abbreviations, word-sheet
app: grammar-line
short: Each word in the Bible has a label naming its grammar, such as “Verb aorist active indicative 3rd singular”. This guide explains every word in them.

STEPBible tags every word of the Greek and Hebrew with a short code, such as V-AAI-3S for a Greek verb or HVqp3ms for a Hebrew verb. The app turns each code into words: “Verb aorist active indicative 3rd singular”. Under each word in the Reader there is a shorter version with abbreviations, such as “verb aor. act. ind. 3rd sg.”: see [[abbreviations]].

A label lists the facts a reader needs in order to understand the word: what kind of word it is ([[word-classes|its part of speech]]), and then, for a verb, its [[tense]] or form, [[voice]], [[mood]] and [[person]], and for a noun its [[case]], [[number]] and [[gender]].

Below most labels the app adds STEPBible's plain description and a sample English phrase, such as “he taught”. These samples use made-up example words to show the shape of the meaning, not the meaning of this word.

Tap “What do these words mean?” under any grammar in the app to see each part explained.

## how-common | How common a word is
aka: how common, count, frequency, occurrences, times
see: strongs, review-scheduling
count: kai grc key=^G2532$
short: The number of times a dictionary word appears in the New Testament or Old Testament text the app uses.

Under each word's details, and in the Words tab, the app counts how often its [[dictionary-form]] appears. A small number of words make up most of the text, so learning the common words first pays off quickly. καί, “and”, appears {{kai}} times in the New Testament.

The Review tab's word lists are built from these counts, most common first.
