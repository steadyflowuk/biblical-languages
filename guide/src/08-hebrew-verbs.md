# hebrew-verbs | Hebrew verbs

A Hebrew verb is built from three things: a root that gives the basic meaning, a stem pattern that gives the kind of action (simple, intensive, causative, passive), and a form with prefixes or endings that shows who does it and how the action is viewed. This section explains each, in the order a grammar label lists them.

## hebrew-verb | How a Hebrew verb is built
lang: he
aka: Hebrew verb, verb parts, parsing a Hebrew verb, binyan
see: root, stems, perfect-hebrew, imperfect-hebrew, wayyiqtol, pgn
short: A Hebrew verb is its root, shaped by a stem pattern, in one of a few forms, with prefixes or endings that show the person: וַיֹּאמֶר, “and he said”.

A grammar label for a Hebrew verb reads like this: “Verb: Qal (simple, active) consecutive imperfect (past/present indicative) third singular masculine”. Taking it in order:

- the stem, here [[qal]], with STEPBible's short description of it in brackets: see [[stems]];
- the form, here the “consecutive imperfect”, better known as the [[wayyiqtol]], with a note on its usual time: see [[time-in-labels|how labels show time]];
- the [[person]], [[number]] and [[gender]]: “he”. The app shortens this to 3ms: see [[pgn]].

The main forms are a handful. The [[perfect-hebrew|perfect]] (qatal) uses endings: שָׁמַרְתִּי, “I kept”. The [[imperfect-hebrew|imperfect]] (yiqtol) uses prefixes and sometimes endings: יִשְׁמֹר, “he will keep”. Joined to “and”, each of these has a narrative use: see [[wayyiqtol]] and [[weqatal]]. There are also commands ([[imperative]], [[jussive]], [[cohortative]]), two infinitives ([[infinitive-construct]], [[infinitive-absolute]]) and the [[hebrew-participle|participle]].

## hebrew-word-order | Word order in Hebrew
lang: he
aka: word order, verb first, VSO, sentence order, Hebrew sentences
see: wayyiqtol, verbless-clause, adjective, hebrew-verb, sentence-parts
short: In a Hebrew story the verb usually comes first, then the subject, then the rest: וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים, “and God said”, is literally “and said God”.

English puts the subject first: “God said”. Hebrew prose usually starts with the verb, especially the [[wayyiqtol]]: וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלֹהִים יְהִי אוֹר, “And God said, ‘Let there be light’” ([GEN 1:3]), word for word “and said God, let there be light”. The [[object]] usually comes after the subject.

When something else comes first, it is often for emphasis, a contrast, or a fresh start: [GEN 1:1] puts “in the beginning” before the verb: בְּרֵאשִׁית בָּרָא אֱלֹהִים, word for word “in the beginning created God”. A Hebrew adjective follows its noun ([[adjective]]), and a sentence can have no verb at all ([[verbless-clause]]). Poetry moves words around much more freely.

## stems | Verb stems: Qal, Piel, Hiphil and the rest
lang: he
aka: stem, stems, binyan, binyanim, verb pattern, simple, intensive, causative, reflexive
match: simple=the plain meaning of the root; intensive/resultive/transitive=a stronger, resulting or “making” sense; causative/declarative=causing someone to do it, or declaring it so; reflexive/iterative=doing it to oneself, or again and again
see: qal, niphal, piel, pual, hiphil, hophal, hithpael, root, voice
short: A Hebrew root can be used in several patterns, called stems, that change the kind of action: simple, passive, intensive, causative or reflexive.

From one [[root]], Hebrew can make up to seven common verbs by fitting it into different patterns of vowels, prefixes and doubled letters. Each pattern is a stem (in Hebrew, *binyan*, “building”). Not every root is used in every stem, and the meanings are tendencies rather than rules: the dictionary gives each stem's meaning for each verb.

Table: the seven common stems, with the root קדשׁ, “holy”
| stem | its usual kind of action | example |
| [[qal]] | simple, active | קָדַשׁ, “he was holy” |
| [[niphal]] | simple, passive or reflexive | נִקְדַּשׁ, “he was shown to be holy” |
| [[piel]] | intensive or “making” | קִדַּשׁ, “he made holy, consecrated” |
| [[pual]] | passive of the piel | מְקֻדָּשׁ, “consecrated” |
| [[hiphil]] | causative | הִקְדִּישׁ, “he set apart as holy” |
| [[hophal]] | passive of the hiphil | the root קדשׁ has none |
| [[hithpael]] | reflexive | הִתְקַדְּשׁוּ, “they sanctified themselves” |

STEPBible's labels put a short description after each stem name, such as “Hiphil (causative/declarative, active)”. The descriptions say what the stem usually does: *simple*, *intensive/resultive/transitive* (a stronger action, or one that brings about a state; “transitive” means the verb takes an [[object]]), *causative/declarative* (making someone do something, or declaring something so), and *reflexive/iterative* (doing it to oneself, or repeatedly). The word “active” or “passive” after the description is the [[voice]]: whether the subject does the action or has it done to it. The app shows them as STEPBible writes them.

A few [[rare-stems]] are explained in their own entry, and the Aramaic stems in [[aramaic-stems]].

## qal | Qal: the simple stem
lang: he
aka: Qal, kal, G stem, simple stem
match: qal=the simple stem
see: stems, niphal, dictionary-form
find: he (^|/)V[q]
short: The basic stem of a Hebrew verb, with its plain meaning: שָׁמַר, “he kept”. Most verbs in the Bible are Qal.

Qal means “light”, as in “simple”. It gives a root its plain meaning: אָמַר, “he said”, הָלַךְ, “he went”, שָׁמַר, “he kept”. Dictionaries list verbs by their Qal [[perfect-hebrew|perfect]] form, “he did”, so the Qal is also the form you look up.

Its passive [[participle]] is labelled “Qal (simple, passive)”: בָּרוּךְ, “blessed”.

## niphal | Niphal
lang: he
aka: Niphal, nifal, N stem
match: niphal=the passive or reflexive of the simple stem
see: stems, qal, passive
find: he (^|/)VN
short: Usually the passive of the Qal: “he was kept”. Sometimes reflexive (“he hid himself”) or with a meaning of its own. It is marked by a נ at the front.

The Niphal adds נ before the root, which shows in the [[perfect-hebrew|perfect]]: נִשְׁמַר, “he was kept” or “he took care”. In other forms the נ merges into the next letter and leaves a doubling dot ([[dagesh]]): הִשָּׁמֶר, “be careful!”, literally “keep yourself”.

Most Niphal verbs are passive: נִקְרָא, “he was called”. Some are reflexive, “he hid himself”, and some simply have their own meaning, such as נִלְחַם, “he fought”.

## piel | Piel
lang: he
aka: Piel, D stem, intensive stem, doubled stem
match: piel=the stem with a doubled middle letter: often “to make” or a stronger action
see: stems, pual, dagesh
find: he (^|/)Vp
short: A stem marked by a doubled middle letter of the root. It often means “to make something so” or a stronger, repeated action: קִדַּשׁ, “he made holy”.

The Piel doubles the middle letter of the root, shown by a [[dagesh]] in it: דִּבֶּר, with בּ. Older grammars called it the intensive stem. Scholars now describe it more as bringing about a state: from “to be holy”, Qal, comes “to make holy”, Piel. For many verbs it is just the usual stem: דִּבֶּר, “he spoke”, is Piel, and there is hardly any Qal of that verb.

## pual | Pual
lang: he
aka: Pual, Dp stem, passive intensive
match: pual=the passive of the piel
see: piel, stems, passive
find: he (^|/)VP
short: The passive of the [[piel]]: “it was made holy”. Like the Piel it doubles the middle letter, with a u-vowel at the start.

The Pual is not common. It has the Piel's doubled middle letter and a u-vowel (ֻ, called qibbuts) under the first: מְקֻדָּשׁ, “consecrated”. Many Pual forms in the Bible are [[participle|participles]].

## hiphil | Hiphil
lang: he
aka: Hiphil, hifil, H stem, causative stem
match: hiphil=the causative stem: “to make someone do”
see: hophal, stems, qal
find: he (^|/)Vh
short: The causative stem: “to make someone do” the root's action. From “to come out” comes “to bring out”. It is marked by a ה at the front and often a י inside.

The Hiphil turns a verb of doing into a verb of causing: יָצָא, “he went out”, Qal; הוֹצִיא, “he brought out”, Hiphil. Its [[perfect-hebrew|perfect]] starts with ה: הִשְׁמִיד, “he destroyed”. In other forms the ה is hidden after a prefix, but a long i-vowel, written with י, often shows before the last letter: יַשְׁמִיד, “he will destroy”.

Some Hiphil verbs declare rather than cause: הִצְדִּיקוּ, “they declare righteous”, from the root for “righteous”. That is why STEPBible's description says “causative/declarative”.

## hophal | Hophal
lang: he
aka: Hophal, hofal, Hp stem
match: hophal=the passive of the hiphil
see: hiphil, stems, passive
find: he (^|/)VH
short: The passive of the [[hiphil]]: “he was made to do”, “he was brought”. It is marked by an o or u vowel at the start.

The Hophal is rare. It is the [[hiphil]] turned passive: “he brought”, Hiphil; “he was brought”, Hophal. Its first syllable usually has a short o or u: הֻגַּד, “it was told”.

## hithpael | Hithpael
lang: he
aka: Hithpael, hitpael, HtD stem, reflexive stem
match: hithpael=the reflexive stem: doing it to oneself
see: piel, stems, reflexive
find: he (^|/)Vt
short: The reflexive stem: doing the action to oneself or with each other, or going about doing it: הִתְקַדְּשׁוּ, “they sanctified themselves”, הִתְפַּלֵּל, “he prayed”.

The Hithpael adds הִתְ- at the front and doubles the middle letter like the [[piel]]. Its meaning is often reflexive (“to sanctify oneself”), sometimes doing something with one another, or repeatedly, or pretending: הִתְהַלֵּךְ, “he walked about”. הִתְפַּלֵּל, “he prayed”, is one of its commonest words.

When the root starts with ס, שׂ or שׁ, the ת swaps places with it: הִשְׁתַּמֵּר, “he kept himself”, not הִתְשַׁמֵּר. After צ the ת also becomes ט, and before ד, ט or ת it merges into a doubled letter.

unattested: הִתְשַׁמֵּר, הִשְׁתַּמֵּר

## rare-stems | Rare stems
lang: he
aka: hishtaphel, tiphil, nithpael, hothpaal, rare stem, minor stems, polel, pilpel
match: hishtaphel=a rare causative-reflexive stem, used for “bowing down”; tiphil=a rare causative stem with ת; nithpael=a rare passive of the hithpael; hothpaal=a rare passive of the hithpael
see: stems, hithpael
find: he (^|/)V[vcDu]
short: A handful of verbs use stems outside the main seven. The best known is “to bow down” or “to worship”, הִשְׁתַּחֲוָה, labelled hishtaphel.

הִשְׁתַּחֲוָה, “he bowed down”, used for bowing to people and for worshipping God, is the commonest of these. Older grammars explained it as a reflexive form of a root שׁחה. Most now see it as the rare hishtaphel stem of the root חוה, and STEPBible labels it so.

The tiphil, nithpael and hothpaal appear only a few times each. When you meet one, the [[dictionary-form]] and the English in the verse are your guide.

Grammars also name patterns such as the polel and pilpel, in which a root with a weak middle letter repeats a letter to do the work of the [[piel]] (see [[weak-verbs]]). STEPBible counts these as Piel or [[hithpael|Hithpael]], so the app's labels don't use those names.

## perfect-hebrew | Perfect (qatal)
lang: he
aka: qatal, perfect, suffix conjugation, pf., Hebrew perfect, past tense in Hebrew
match: perfect=the qatal form: an action seen as complete
see: imperfect-hebrew, weqatal, aspect, time-in-labels
find: he (^|/)V[a-zA-Z]p
short: The Hebrew verb form with endings, qatal, usually for a completed action, which English mostly translates with the past: שָׁמַר, “he kept”.

The perfect is made by adding endings to the stem: שָׁמַר, “he kept”, שָׁמַרְתִּי, “I kept”, שָׁמְרוּ, “they kept”. Grammar books call it qatal, after the pattern קָטַל, “he killed”, the verb older grammars used as their model. They also call it the suffix conjugation (a conjugation is a set of verb forms), because the person is shown by a suffix.

It presents an action as complete. Usually that means the past, but not always: with verbs of state it can be present (יָדַעְתִּי, “I know”), and in promises and prophecy something so certain that it is spoken of as done. That is why STEPBible labels it “past/present”.

Joined to “and”, it often carries on a series of future or commanded actions: see [[weqatal]].

Table: the endings of the perfect, on שָׁמַר
| who | form |
| he | שָׁמַר |
| she | שָׁמְרָה |
| you (a man) | שָׁמַרְתָּ |
| you (a woman) | שָׁמַרְתְּ |
| I | שָׁמַרְתִּי |
| they | שָׁמְרוּ |
| you (men, more than one) | שְׁמַרְתֶּם |
| we | שָׁמַרְנוּ |

unattested: שָׁמַרְתְּ, שְׁמַרְתֶּם, שָׁמְרָה

## imperfect-hebrew | Imperfect (yiqtol)
lang: he
aka: yiqtol, imperfect, prefix conjugation, impf., Hebrew imperfect, future in Hebrew
match: imperfect=the yiqtol form: an action seen as not complete
see: perfect-hebrew, wayyiqtol, jussive, cohortative, aspect, time-in-labels
find: he (^|/)V[a-zA-Z]i avoid=PSA106:12
short: The Hebrew verb form with prefixes, yiqtol, for actions not seen as complete: usually the future, but also habits, wishes and commands: יִשְׁמֹר, “he will keep”.

The imperfect adds a prefix that shows the person (א for “I”, ת for “you” and “she”, י for “he” and “they”, נ for “we”), and sometimes an ending too: יִשְׁמֹר, “he will keep”, תִּשְׁמְרוּ, “you will keep”. Grammar books call it yiqtol, or the prefix conjugation.

It presents an action as not complete. That can be the future (“he will keep”), something that happens again and again (“he used to keep”), something possible or wished (“he may keep”, “let him keep”), or a lasting command: לֹא תִּגְנֹב, “you shall not steal”. That range is why STEPBible's labels say “future/present” and sometimes add “indicative/jussive”.

Don't confuse it with the Greek [[imperfect-greek|imperfect]], which is a past [[tense]]. In stories, an imperfect with a special “and” in front is the main past form: see [[wayyiqtol]].

Table: the prefixes and endings of the imperfect, on שָׁמַר
| who | form |
| he | יִשְׁמֹר |
| she, you (one) | תִּשְׁמֹר |
| I | אֶשְׁמֹר |
| they | יִשְׁמְרוּ |
| you (more than one) | תִּשְׁמְרוּ |
| we | נִשְׁמֹר |

## wayyiqtol | Wayyiqtol: the narrative past
lang: he
aka: wayyiqtol, vayyiqtol, waw consecutive, vav consecutive imperfect, consecutive imperfect, narrative past, preterite, and he said
match: consecutive imperfect=the wayyiqtol: “and he did”, the narrative past
see: imperfect-hebrew, conjunction-vav, weqatal, perfect-hebrew, hebrew-word-order
lessons: he-9
find: he (^|/)V[a-zA-Z]w n=3
short: “And” joined to an [[imperfect-hebrew|imperfect]] form, used for the main events of a story in the past: וַיֹּאמֶר, “and he said”. It is the commonest verb form in Hebrew stories.

Biblical stories move forward with this form: וַיְהִי, “and it came to pass”, וַיֹּאמֶר, “and he said”, וַיֵּלֶךְ, “and he went”. It looks like an [[imperfect-hebrew|imperfect]] with “and” in front, but it describes a completed past event, one after another in the story.

You can recognise it by the “and”: וַ, with a patah and a doubled letter after it (though a י with a [[shewa]] usually drops the doubling dot: וַיְהִי). Before א the vowel lengthens instead: וָאֹמַר, “and I said”. Its stress often moves towards the front of the word, which can shorten its last vowel.

STEPBible calls it the “consecutive imperfect”. The app's short label is “wayyiqtol”, the name most grammars now use. The name starts with w, as ו was probably said in the earliest times; the [[masoretes|Masoretes]], and the app, say v: va-YO-mer.

## weqatal | Weqatal: “and he will”
lang: he
aka: weqatal, vav consecutive perfect, consecutive perfect, waw perfect, and you shall
match: consecutive perfect=the weqatal: “and he will”, carrying on a future or command
see: perfect-hebrew, wayyiqtol, conjunction-vav, imperfect-hebrew
find: he (^|/)V[a-zA-Z]q n=3
short: “And” joined to a [[perfect-hebrew|perfect]] form, used to carry on a series of future, habitual or commanded actions: וְאָמַרְתָּ, “and you shall say”.

Where the [[wayyiqtol]] carries a story forward in the past, the weqatal carries forward a series of actions that are future, repeated or commanded. After a command or a future verb, each following step is often a weqatal: “go, and you shall say to them…”.

It looks like a [[perfect-hebrew|perfect]] with an ordinary וְ in front. Context shows the difference between it and a plain “and he did”. STEPBible calls it the “consecutive perfect”, and its labels mark the time “future/present”.

## weyiqtol | Weyiqtol: “and” with an imperfect
lang: he
aka: weyiqtol, conjunctive imperfect, conjunction+imperfect, and let him
match: conjunction+imperfect=an imperfect with an ordinary “and” in front (weyiqtol)
see: imperfect-hebrew, wayyiqtol, jussive
find: he (^|/)V[a-zA-Z]u n=2
short: An [[imperfect-hebrew|imperfect]] with an ordinary “and” in front, often expressing purpose or a wish: “and let him go”, “so that he may go”.

This is not the narrative [[wayyiqtol]]: its “and” is the ordinary וְ, with no doubled letter after it. It often follows a command and gives its purpose, “do this, so that…”, or continues a wish. STEPBible labels it “conjunction+imperfect”.

## jussive | Jussive: “let him”
lang: he
aka: jussive, let him, may he, third person command, short imperfect
match: jussive=a wish or command for someone else: “let him”, “may it”
see: imperfect-hebrew, cohortative, imperative, prohibition
find: he (^|/)V[a-zA-Z]j[23] n=3 avoid=PSA107:29
short: A form of the [[imperfect-hebrew|imperfect]] used to wish or command something of another person: יְהִי אוֹר, “let there be light”.

The jussive expresses the speaker's will for someone other than the one spoken to: “let him go”, “may the LORD bless you”. It looks like the [[imperfect-hebrew|imperfect]], and for most verbs it is exactly the same; for some it is a little shorter: יִהְיֶה, “it will be”, but יְהִי, “let it be”. With אַל in front it means “don't”: see [[prohibition]].

> GEN 1:3 יְהִי | יְהִי אוֹר, “let there be light”: the short form of יִהְיֶה

STEPBible's labels mark it “future/present jussive”, or “indicative/jussive” where the form could be either.

## cohortative | Cohortative: “let me”, “let us”
lang: he
aka: cohortative, let us, let me, first person wish
match: cohortative=a wish or resolve for oneself: “let me”, “let us”
see: jussive, imperative, paragogic
find: he (^|/)V[a-zA-Z]c1 n=3
short: A form of the [[imperfect-hebrew|imperfect]] for the speaker's own wish or resolve: “let me go”, “let us go”, often with an extra ה at the end: נֵלְכָה, “let us go”.

The cohortative is the “I” and “we” partner of the [[imperative]] and [[jussive]]. It often has the [[paragogic|extra ending]] -ָה: אֵלְכָה, “let me go”, נֵלְכָה, “let us go”. Without that ending it may look just like an ordinary “I will” or “we will”, and only the context shows the wish.

## infinitive-construct | Infinitive construct
lang: he
aka: infinitive construct, inf. cstr., Hebrew infinitive, to-form in Hebrew, when he went
match: infinitive construct=the “to …” form of a Hebrew verb, also “when …”
see: infinitive, infinitive-absolute, hebrew-prepositions
find: he (^|/)V[a-zA-Z]cc n=3
short: The Hebrew [[infinitive]] used like English “to …”, often with a preposition: לִשְׁמֹר, “to keep”; with בְּ or כְּ it means “when”: בְּבֹאוֹ, “when he came”.

The infinitive construct is the form most like an English [[infinitive]]. With לְ in front it means “to” or “in order to”: לֵאמֹר, literally “to say”, introduces speech like a colon, and English translates it “saying”. With בְּ or כְּ in front and a pronoun ending, it makes a time clause: “when he came”, “as they went”.

## infinitive-absolute | Infinitive absolute
lang: he
aka: infinitive absolute, inf. abs., emphatic infinitive, surely
match: infinitive absolute=a form of the verb used to strengthen another verb: “surely”
see: infinitive, infinitive-construct
find: he (^|/)V[a-zA-Z]aa n=3
short: A form of the verb, often placed before another form of the same verb for emphasis: מוֹת תָּמוּת, “you will surely die”.

The infinitive absolute is an unusual [[infinitive]]: it doesn't change, takes no prefixes or endings, and is mostly used beside another verb from the same root, to make it stronger. מוֹת תָּמוּת, “dying you will die”, means “you will surely die” ([GEN 2:17]). English translates the effect with “surely”, “indeed” or “certainly”. It can also stand in for a command: זָכוֹר, “remember!”.

## hebrew-participle | Hebrew participles
lang: he
aka: Hebrew participle, participle passive, active participle, passive participle, ptc., one who
match: participle=a verb used like an adjective or noun: “going”, “one who goes”; participle passive=a participle with a passive meaning: “kept”, “blessed”
see: participle, qal, verbless-clause
find: he (^|/)V[a-zA-Z][rs] n=3 avoid=PSA113:6
short: A verb used like an adjective or noun, for ongoing action or for someone who does something: שֹׁמֵר, “keeping”, “a keeper”; בָּרוּךְ, “blessed”.

The Hebrew [[participle]] often describes something going on at the time: הִנֵּה אָנֹכִי עֹמֵד, “look, I am standing”. As a noun it names someone by what they do: שֹׁמֵר, “a keeper”, רֹעֶה, “a shepherd”. It has the [[gender]] and [[number]] of an [[adjective]], not a [[person]], so “I am standing” and “he is standing” use the same word.

The Qal passive participle has a u-vowel: בָּרוּךְ, “blessed”, כָּתוּב, “written”. STEPBible labels it “participle passive”.

## time-in-labels | “Past/present” and “future/present” in Hebrew labels
lang: he
aka: past/present, future/present, indicative/jussive, indicative/cohortative, time in Hebrew labels
match: past/present indicative=a statement, usually about the past (or the present); future/present indicative=a statement, usually about the future (or the present); future/present jussive=a wish or command for someone else; future/present cohortative=a wish or resolve of the speaker; future/present indicative/jussive=a statement or a wish: the form can be either; future/present indicative/cohortative=a statement or a resolve: the form can be either
see: perfect-hebrew, imperfect-hebrew, aspect, jussive, cohortative
short: STEPBible's Hebrew labels add a note in brackets on a verb's usual time and use. “Past/present” and “future/present” mean either may be right.

Hebrew verb forms don't fix time the way English tenses do: see [[aspect]]. So STEPBible's labels add a reminder in brackets. “Past/present indicative” after a [[perfect-hebrew|perfect]] or [[wayyiqtol]] means a statement, usually about the past. “Future/present indicative” after an [[imperfect-hebrew|imperfect]] or [[weqatal]] means a statement, usually about the future.

Where the form could be a statement or a wish, the label says so: “indicative/jussive” or “indicative/cohortative”. In each case, the English in the verse shows how the translators read it.

## pgn | Person, gender and number: 3ms, 2fp and the like
lang: he
aka: 3ms, 3fs, 3mp, 3fp, 2ms, 2fs, 2mp, 2fp, 1cs, 1cp, 3cp, pgn, PGN, ms, fs, mp, fp, cs, cp, md, fd
see: person, gender, number, abbreviations, pronoun-suffix
short: Under Hebrew words the app shortens person, gender and number to three characters: 3ms means “he” (3rd person, masculine, singular); 1cp means “we”.

The digit is the [[person]]: 1 “I/we”, 2 “you”, 3 “he/she/they”. The first letter is the [[gender]]: m masculine, f feminine, c common (either). The last is the [[number]]: s singular, p plural, d [[dual]]. So:

Table: the commonest combinations
| short | means |
| 3ms | he, him, his |
| 3fs | she, her |
| 3mp | they (men), them |
| 3cp | they (anyone) |
| 2ms | you (a man) |
| 2fs | you (a woman) |
| 2mp | you (men, more than one) |
| 1cs | I, me, my |
| 1cp | we, us, our |

Nouns and participles have no person, so they show only gender and number: ms, fp. The same letters appear on [[pronoun-suffix|pronoun endings]]: “suffix 3ms” is “his” or “him”.

## weak-verbs | Weak verbs: roots with a missing or changing letter
lang: he
aka: weak verb, weak verbs, irregular verbs, hollow verb, III-he, I-nun, guttural verbs, weak letter
see: root, gutturals, stems
short: Roots that contain א, ה, ו, י or נ, or a throat letter, often lose or change a letter: נָתַן, “he gave”, becomes יִתֵּן, “he will give”, without its first נ.

Regular verbs keep all three root letters in every form. Many common verbs don't. A נ at the start often vanishes and leaves a doubled letter ([[dagesh]]): יִתֵּן from נתן. A ה at the end often disappears or becomes another vowel: בָּנָה, “he built”, וַיִּבֶן, “and he built”. A ו or י in the middle can turn into a vowel: קוּם, “to rise”, קָם, “he rose”. [[gutturals|Throat letters]] change the vowels around them.

Grammars name these verbs by the position of the weak letter: I-nun means a נ first (נתן), III-he a ה last (בנה), and a hollow verb has ו or י in the middle (קום).

When a word seems to have only two root letters, it is probably one of these. The app always shows the [[dictionary-form]], so you can see the full root.
