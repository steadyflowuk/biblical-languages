# hebrew-writing | Hebrew letters, vowels and signs

Hebrew is written from right to left, with consonant letters and small marks for the vowels and accents. A page of the Hebrew Bible can look crowded, but each mark has one job. This section explains them one by one. The Letters tab shows every letter and vowel with its sound.

## hebrew-alphabet | The Hebrew alphabet
lang: he
aka: alphabet, Hebrew letters, aleph bet, alef bet, right to left
see: final-letters, vowel-points, dagesh, gutturals
lessons: he-1, he-2
short: Hebrew has twenty-two letters, all consonants, written from right to left. Five of them have a different shape at the end of a word.

The alphabet runs from א, aleph, to ת, tav. Every letter is a consonant: the vowels are shown by marks added around the letters ([[vowel-points]]). Two letters, א and ע, are silent in the app's pronunciation, and ו and י can also stand for vowels ([[matres]]).

Several letters look alike and are worth practising in pairs: ב and כ, ד and ר, ה and ח, ו and ז, ס and ם. The [[#/alphabet/he|Letters tab]] shows each letter with its name, its sound and words to hear.

## final-letters | Final letters
lang: he
aka: final forms, final letters, sofit, ך ם ן ף ץ
see: hebrew-alphabet
lessons: he-2
short: Five letters change shape at the end of a word: כ becomes ך, מ becomes ם, נ becomes ן, פ becomes ף, and צ becomes ץ. The sound stays the same.

The final shapes mostly reach down below the line, or close up. They only mark the end of a word: מֶלֶךְ, “king”, ends in ך; the plural מְלָכִים has the ordinary כ in the middle and ם at the end. When you search the [[words-tab|Words tab]], the app treats each letter and its final form as the same letter.

## vowel-points | Vowel points
lang: he
aka: vowels, vowel points, nikkud, niqqud, pointing, patah, qamets, segol, tsere, hiriq, holam, qibbuts, shureq
see: masoretes, shewa, hateph, qamets-hatuf, matres
lessons: he-3
short: The dots and dashes under, over and beside Hebrew letters that show the vowels. They were added by the [[masoretes]]; the letters themselves are consonants.

Each vowel mark is said after the letter it is attached to: בָּ is “ba”, בִּ is “bi”. The vowels fall into three families:

Table: the vowels, on ב
| family | mark | name | sound |
| a | בַ | patah | a, as in “father”, short |
| a | בָ | qamets | a, as in “father” (sometimes o: see [[qamets-hatuf]]) |
| e | בֶ | segol | e, as in “bed” |
| e | בֵ | tsere | e, a little longer |
| i | בִ | hiriq | i, as in “machine” |
| o | בֹ | holam | o, as in “go” |
| u | בֻ | qibbuts | u, as in “rule” |

unattested: בַ, בָ, בֶ, בֵ, בִ, בֹ, בֻ

Some vowels are written with a letter as well: וֹ (holam with ו) is o, וּ (shureq) is u, and ִי is i. See [[matres]]. There is also a mark for “no vowel” or a very short one: the [[shewa]].

The Masoretes added these marks to a text that was already centuries old, to record exactly how it was read. That is why one Hebrew word can have a dot inside a letter, a vowel under it and an accent too.

## matres | Vowel letters: ה, ו and י
lang: he
aka: matres lectionis, mater lectionis, vowel letters, full spelling, defective spelling, plene
see: vowel-points, hebrew-alphabet
short: The letters ה, ו and י sometimes stand for a vowel instead of a consonant: וֹ is o, וּ is u, ִי is i, and a final ה after a vowel is silent.

Before vowel marks existed, scribes used a few letters to hint at vowels, and they still do the job: in טוֹב, “good”, the ו is the vowel o; in דָּוִד, “David”, the ו is a consonant, v. A ה at the end of a word after a vowel is silent, as in תּוֹרָה, “law”, unless it has a dot ([[dagesh|mappiq]]).

The same word may be spelt with or without the vowel letter: לֹא and לוֹא are both “not”. Grammars call these full and short (or plene and defective) spellings. The meaning is the same.

## dagesh | Dagesh: the dot inside a letter
lang: he
aka: dagesh, dagesh lene, dagesh forte, mappiq, rafe, doubling dot, dot in a letter
see: begadkefat, gutturals, hebrew-article, piel
lessons: he-5
short: A dot inside a letter. In ב, כ and פ it makes the hard sound (b, k, p); after a vowel it doubles the letter: הַשָּׁמַיִם has a doubled שׁ.

The same dot does three jobs:

- in the six letters ב ג ד כ פ ת at the start of a word or after a consonant, it marks the hard sound: בּ is b, without the dot ב is v. This is the dagesh lene (“light”); see [[begadkefat]];
- after a vowel, it doubles the letter: הַשָּׁמַיִם is hash-sha-MA-yim, “the heavens”. This is the dagesh forte (“strong”), and it often shows that something has been added, such as the article “the”, or that a letter has dropped out;
- in a final ה it shows the ה is sounded, not silent: לָהּ, “to her”. This dot is called mappiq.

A small line over a letter, called rafe, marks that a letter has no dagesh. The Leningrad Codex uses it often, and the Reader shows it.

## begadkefat | The six letters with two sounds: ב ג ד כ פ ת
lang: he
aka: begadkefat, begadkephat, bgdkpt, spirant, hard and soft letters
see: dagesh, hebrew-pronunciation
lessons: he-2, he-5
short: Six letters, ב ג ד כ פ ת, have a hard sound with a dot inside (b, g, d, k, p, t) and a soft one without it. In the app's pronunciation only ב, כ and פ change: to v, kh and f.

The six are remembered by the made-up word begadkefat. With a [[dagesh]] they are hard: בּ b, כּ k, פּ p. Without it, after a vowel, they are soft: ב v, כ kh (as in Scottish “loch”), פ f. So the same root can be heard two ways: כָּתַב, ka-TAV, “he wrote”, and יִכְתֹּב, yikh-TOV, “he will write”.

In ancient times ג, ד and ת also had soft sounds (gh, th as in “this”, th as in “thin”). Modern pronunciation, and the app's, says them the same with or without the dot.

## shewa | Shewa: said and silent
lang: he
aka: shewa, sheva, schwa, vocal shewa, silent shewa, said shewa, ְ
see: vowel-points, hateph, syllables, hebrew-pronunciation
lessons: he-4
short: Two dots under a letter, one above the other (בְ). At the start of a syllable it is said as a very short e; at the end of one it is silent.

The shewa marks a letter with either a very short vowel or none at all. The rule of thumb: it is said (“vocal”) where it starts a [[syllables|syllable]], and silent where it closes one.

- At the start of a word it is said: בְּרִית, be-RIT, “covenant”.
- After a short vowel, closing the syllable, it is silent: יִשְׁמֹר, yish-MOR.
- Of two shewas in a row, the second is said: יִשְׁמְרוּ, yish-me-RU.
- Under a doubled letter, it is said: הַמְּלָכִים, ham-me-la-KHIM.
- After a long vowel, it is said: יָלְדָה, ya-le-DAH, “she bore”.

In everyday Israeli Hebrew many said shewas are dropped (brit instead of be-RIT). The app follows the academic pronunciation and says them, and its Hebrew voice is given a short e wherever the shewa is said. Under the [[gutturals|throat letters]], a said shewa is replaced by a [[hateph|hateph]] vowel.

## hateph | Hateph vowels: a quick a, e or o
lang: he
aka: hateph, hatef, hateph patah, hateph segol, hateph qamets, composite shewa, half vowel, reduced vowel
see: shewa, gutturals, vowel-points
lessons: he-4
short: A [[shewa]] joined to another vowel makes a very quick a, e or o: אֲנִי, a-NI; אֱלֹהִים, e-lo-HIM; חֳלִי, cho-LI. They are used mostly under the [[gutturals|throat letters]], which can't take an ordinary said shewa.

Where another letter would have a said shewa, the throat letters א ה ח ע usually take a hateph (“hurried”) vowel instead: אֱלֹהִים, e-lo-HIM, “God”; אֲנִי, a-NI, “I”; חֳלִי, cho-LI, “sickness”. Say them like the full vowel, but quickly.

## gutturals | Throat letters: א ה ח ע
lang: he
aka: gutturals, guttural letters, laryngeals, throat letters, א ה ח ע ר
see: dagesh, hateph, furtive-patah, hebrew-article
lessons: he-4
short: The four letters made in the throat, א ה ח ע. They never take the doubling dot, prefer a-vowels, and take a hateph vowel instead of a said [[shewa]]. ר behaves like them in some ways.

Because these letters are made at the back of the throat, Hebrew changes the vowels around them. They can't be doubled, so a vowel before them often lengthens instead: “the” is הָ in הָאָרֶץ, “the earth”, not הַ with a doubling dot. They like a-vowels, which is why a word such as נַעַר, “young man”, has a where מֶלֶךְ, “king”, has e. And they take [[hateph|hateph]] vowels instead of a said shewa.

ר is not made in the throat, but it also refuses the doubling dot. Most grammars count it among the gutturals for that reason; the app's lessons treat it on its own.

## furtive-patah | Furtive patah
lang: he
aka: furtive patah, patah furtivum, stolen patah, sneaky a
see: gutturals, vowel-points
lessons: he-4
short: A patah under a final ח, ע or הּ after another vowel is said before the letter, not after it: רוּחַ is RU-ach, “spirit”.

After a vowel such as u, o or e, it is hard to say a throat letter at the very end of a word. So Hebrew slips in a short a just before it: רוּחַ, RU-ach, not ru-CHA; גָּבֹהַּ, ga-VO-ah, “high”. The patah under the last letter is written there, but said before it. It is never stressed.

## qamets-hatuf | Qamets hatuf: the qamets said o
lang: he
aka: qamets hatuf, qamets chatuf, kamatz katan, short o, ׇ
see: vowel-points, syllables, hebrew-pronunciation
lessons: he-3
short: The mark ָ is usually a (qamets), but in a closed, unstressed syllable it is a short o: כָּל־ is kol, “all”; חָכְמָה is chokh-MAH, “wisdom”.

Hebrew uses one mark for two sounds. In most places ָ is a long a. But where it stands in a syllable that ends in a consonant and has no stress, it is a short o, called qamets hatuf: כָּל־הָאָרֶץ, kol ha-A-rets, “all the earth”. Some editions print the short o with a slightly longer stem (ׇ) to tell them apart, and some dictionary forms in the app use it.

The [[masoretes]] said their qamets like the o in British “law”, which is why one sign could cover both. The app's Hebrew voice gets the o wherever the spelling or the grammar settles it.

## syllables | Syllables and stress in Hebrew
lang: he
aka: syllable, syllables, stress, accent, tone, open syllable, closed syllable, segolate, milra, milel
see: stress, cantillation, shewa, qamets-hatuf
lessons: he-5
short: Most Hebrew words are stressed on the last syllable: da-VAR, “word”. Some, such as מֶלֶךְ, ME-lekh, are stressed on the one before.

A Hebrew syllable almost always starts with a consonant and has one vowel: בָּרָא, “he created”, is two syllables, ba and RA. A syllable that ends in a vowel is open; one that ends in a consonant is closed.

Most words are stressed on the last syllable. A large group of nouns, called segolates because many have two segols, are stressed on the one before: מֶלֶךְ, ME-lekh, “king”; אֶרֶץ, E-rets, “land”; נַעַר, NA-ar, “young man” (a, not e, because of the [[gutturals|throat letter]] ע). Some verb endings are unstressed too. Grammars call stress on the last syllable milra and stress on the one before milel, from Aramaic words for “below” and “above”.

The [[cantillation|accent marks]] show which syllable is stressed. In the app's respellings the stressed syllable is in capitals.

## stress | Stress: the strong syllable
lang: both
aka: stress, stressed syllable, emphasis, capitals in respelling, tonic
see: accents, syllables, respelling, cantillation
short: The syllable said a little louder and longer than the others: the first syllable of “TA-ble”, the second of “a-GO”. The app writes it in capitals.

In English, putting the stress in the wrong place can change a word (“RE-cord” and “re-CORD”). In Greek and Hebrew it matters too, and the texts show it. In Greek the [[accents]] mark the stressed syllable. In Hebrew most words carry an accent mark on the stressed syllable ([[cantillation]]), and the stress usually falls on the last syllable ([[syllables]]).

The app's [[respelling|respellings]] write the stressed syllable in capitals: λόγος LO-gos, דָּבָר da-VAR. The Hebrew voice on your phone chooses its own stress, so it may sometimes stress a word differently from the respelling.

## maqqef | Maqqef: the joining line
lang: he
aka: maqqef, makaf, maqaf, hyphen, ־, joined words
see: construct-state, cantillation, qamets-hatuf
lessons: he-5
short: A short line at the top of the letters (־) that joins two words to be said as one, with one stress: כָּל־הָאָרֶץ, kol ha-A-rets, “all the earth”.

The maqqef works like a hyphen. Words joined by it are read together, and the first word loses its own stress, which can change its vowel: כֹּל, “all”, becomes כָּל־ (kol, with a short o, see [[qamets-hatuf]]). It often joins short words such as כָּל־, אֶת־ and עַל־ to the next word, and links nouns in a [[construct-state|construct]] chain. The Reader shows maqqef-joined words with no space between them.

## cantillation | Accent marks (cantillation)
lang: he
aka: cantillation, accents, teamim, te'amim, trope, Hebrew accents, chanting marks, disjunctive, conjunctive
see: syllables, sof-pasuq, meteg, masoretes
short: Small marks above and below Hebrew words that show the stressed syllable, how the words of a verse group together, and the tune for chanting it aloud.

Almost every word in the Hebrew Bible has one of about thirty accent marks. They do three jobs at once. They show the stressed syllable, because most sit on it. They work as punctuation: some divide the verse (disjunctive accents, like commas and semicolons) and others join a word to the next (conjunctive accents). And they are a musical notation for chanting the text in the synagogue.

You don't need to learn them all. The most useful are the two strongest dividers: atnah (ב֑), under the word that ends the first half of a verse, and silluq (בֽ), on the last word, before the [[sof-pasuq]]. The app's Hebrew voice pauses at both. The [[letters-tab|Letters tab]] shows how they look.

## meteg | Meteg
lang: he
aka: meteg, metheg, gaya, secondary stress, ֽ
see: cantillation, syllables, qamets-hatuf
short: A small upright line beside a vowel (בָֽ) that slows the syllable down. It can show that a qamets is a long a, not an o.

The meteg (“bridle”) marks a syllable that should not be rushed, often two syllables before the stress: הָֽאָדָם, ha-a-DAM, “the man”. Because a long a is said slowly and a short o is not, a meteg beside a qamets is usually a clue that it is a long a: שָֽׁמְרוּ is sha-me-RU, “they kept”. Commands are the exception: in שָֽׁמְרֵנִי, “keep me” ([PSA 16:1]), the qamets is a short o said slowly, sho-me-RE-ni. It looks exactly like silluq ([[cantillation]]), which only comes on the last word of a verse.

## sof-pasuq | Sof pasuq: the end of a verse
lang: he
aka: sof pasuq, sof pasuk, end of verse, ׃, colon
see: cantillation, paseq, petuha-setuma
short: Two dots like a colon (׃) at the end of every verse in the Hebrew Bible. Its name means “end of verse”.

It works like a full stop at the end of the verse. The last word before it also carries silluq, the strongest of the [[cantillation|accent marks]]. The Reader keeps it, and the Hebrew voice stops there.

## paseq | Paseq: a small upright line
lang: he
aka: paseq, pasek, legarmeh, ׀, divider
see: cantillation, sof-pasuq
short: A thin upright line between two words (׀). It keeps them apart in reading, a little like a slight pause.

The paseq (“divider”) stands between words that should not run together, sometimes to avoid slurring two similar sounds, sometimes for emphasis, and it also forms part of some accents. It is rare and doesn't change the meaning. The Reader shows it with a space on each side.

## petuha-setuma | פ and ס: paragraph marks
lang: he
aka: petuha, setuma, open paragraph, closed paragraph, פ, ס, parashah, paragraphs in Hebrew
see: sof-pasuq, paragraph-marks
short: A פ or a ס standing on its own between verses marks the end of a paragraph. They are not words, and the app uses them to start a new paragraph.

The scribes divided the text into sections with gaps on the page: an open section (פְּתוּחָה, petuha) started on a new line, and a closed one (סְתוּמָה, setuma) after a space in the same line. Printed Bibles show these gaps with a single letter, פ or ס. They are older than the chapter and [[verse-numbers|verse numbers]].

The app doesn't print or say these letters. In the running-text layout it starts a new paragraph where they stand.
unattested: פְּתוּחָה, סְתוּמָה

## ketiv-qere | Ketiv and qere: written one way, read another
lang: he
aka: ketiv, kethiv, qere, kere, written and read, °, ketib, ketiv-qere, read differently
see: divine-name, not-read, masoretes
short: In over a thousand places the [[masoretes]] noted that a word is written one way (ketiv) but read another (qere). The app shows the read form, marked °, and gives the written form when you tap it.

The Masoretes would not change a letter of the text they had received, even where it seemed wrong or where the tradition read it differently. Instead they wrote the vowels of the word to be read (the qere, “what is read”) under the letters as written (the ketiv, “what is written”), and noted the reading in the margin.

The app's text shows the qere, the word as it is read aloud, with a small ° after it. Tap the word, and the word sheet shows the ketiv under “Written”. In the Hebrew text the app uses, {{qere}} words are marked this way.

The most frequent case is never marked at all: God's name, see [[divine-name]].
count: qere he flag=q

## divine-name | The name of God: יהוה
lang: he
aka: YHWH, Yahweh, Jehovah, LORD, tetragrammaton, divine name, Adonai, Elohim, the Name
see: ketiv-qere, hebrew-pronunciation
short: God's name, written with four letters, יהוה. By long Jewish custom it is not said aloud: readers say Adonai, “my Lord”, which is why English Bibles print LORD.

The four letters יהוה (Y-H-W-H) are the personal name of God, used thousands of times in the Old Testament. Out of reverence, Jewish readers had stopped saying it aloud by the time of Jesus, and read אֲדֹנָי, Adonai, “my Lord”, instead. The New Testament and most copies of the [[septuagint]] likewise write κύριος, “Lord”, though a few early Septuagint copies keep the four Hebrew letters.

The [[masoretes]] wrote יהוה with vowels taken from Adonai, as a permanent [[ketiv-qere|qere]], so that nobody would say the name. The form “Jehovah” came about when later Christian readers combined those vowels with the letters. Scholars think the name was originally said something like Yahweh.

English Bibles follow the reading custom by printing LORD in small capitals. Where Adonai and יהוה stand together, the name is written with the vowels of אֱלֹהִים, Elohim, “God”, and read Elohim, so English prints “Lord GOD”. The app's Hebrew voice says Adonai or Elohim in the same way.

## not-read | Written but not read
lang: he
aka: written not read, ketiv without qere, grey words, not read aloud
see: ketiv-qere, supplied-words
short: A few words are written in the Hebrew text but, by long tradition, not read aloud. The Reader shows them in grey, and the voice leaves them out.

In a handful of places the [[masoretes]] marked a word to be skipped when reading aloud, usually because it seemed to be written twice or to belong elsewhere. They kept it in the text, without its own vowels for reading. The app shows such words in grey and explains them when tapped. There are {{notread}} of them in the Hebrew text the app uses.
count: notread he flag=k

## supplied-words | Words supplied from other manuscripts
lang: he
aka: supplied, [brackets], not in Leningrad, missing words, x
see: hebrew-text, manuscripts, not-read
short: A few words missing from the Leningrad Codex are added from other manuscripts or old translations. The Reader shows them in square brackets.

Every manuscript has its faults. Where the Leningrad Codex seems to have lost a word or phrase that other Hebrew manuscripts, the Samaritan Pentateuch or early translations such as the [[septuagint]] have, STEPBible supplies it and marks it. The app shows these words in [square brackets] and explains them when tapped. A well-known example is [GEN 4:8], where Cain's words to Abel, “let us go out to the field”, are missing from the Hebrew.

## extraordinary-points | Extraordinary dots over letters
lang: he
aka: extraordinary points, puncta extraordinaria, dots over letters, nequdot
see: masoretes, ketiv-qere
short: In fifteen places, ancient scribes put dots over certain letters or words, probably to mark them as doubtful. The app shows them, but they don't change how the word is read.

The dots are older than the vowel marks, and their meaning was already debated in ancient times. Most likely they marked letters the scribes doubted but would not remove. One example is the dots over וְאַהֲרֹן, “and Aaron”, in [NUM 3:39]. The app ignores them when reading aloud.
unattested: וְאַהֲרֹן

## inverted-nun | Inverted nun: ׆
lang: he
aka: inverted nun, reversed nun, nun hafukha, ׆
see: masoretes, petuha-setuma
short: A reversed letter נ (׆) that stands in a few places, such as around [NUM 10:35] and [NUM 10:36]. It seems to mark a passage as set apart. It is not read.

The inverted nun appears around the “Song of the Ark” in Numbers and several times in [PSA 107:23|Psalm 107]. The ancient rabbis already discussed why: perhaps the passage stood somewhere else, or formed a book on its own. The app shows the sign and doesn't read it aloud.

## psalm-titles | Psalm titles
lang: he
aka: psalm title, superscription, title, heading of a psalm, verse 0
see: verse-numbers
short: Many psalms begin with a title, such as “A Psalm of David”. Hebrew Bibles number it as part of the verses; English Bibles don't number it. The Reader shows it as “title”.

The titles name an author, an occasion, a tune or a musical direction, and they are part of the Hebrew text. In Hebrew Bibles a short title shares verse one with the psalm's first line, so the numbers agree with English Bibles. A longer title is verse one on its own, or even verses one and two ([PSA 51:1|Psalm 51]), and then the psalm's verses are numbered one or two higher than in English Bibles. The app follows English numbering, shows the title at the start labelled “title”, and puts the Hebrew number beside each verse where it differs: see [[verse-numbers]].
