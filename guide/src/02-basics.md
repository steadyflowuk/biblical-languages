# basics | Grammar words everyone needs

Grammar has its own words, and they can make a simple idea sound hard. This section explains the ones you will meet, using English first. Every one of them is something you already use without thinking when you speak English.

Read these in order if grammar is new to you. Each takes a minute or two.

## grammar | What grammar is
aka: grammar words, parts of a sentence, why grammar
see: word-classes, inflection, grammar-labels
short: Grammar is the set of patterns a language uses to show how words fit together and who does what to whom.

In “the dog bit the man”, English shows who did the biting by the order of the words: swap them and the meaning changes. Greek and Hebrew show much of this with changes to the words themselves, mostly with [[inflection|endings]], prefixes and changes of vowel. That is why a Greek or Hebrew word comes with a label naming its grammar.

You don't need to memorise every label. Most of the time you only need to know three things about a word: what kind of word it is (its [[word-classes|part of speech]]), what job it is doing in the sentence, and, for a [[verb]], who is doing it. The entries in this section explain the words used to describe those things.

## word-classes | Parts of speech
aka: part of speech, word class, kinds of word, parts of speech
see: noun, verb, adjective, adverb, pronoun, preposition, conjunction, article, particle, interjection
short: The kinds of word a language has: naming words, doing words, describing words and so on.

Every word belongs to a kind, called its part of speech. The first word of every grammar label in the app tells you which kind it is. The main kinds are:

- a [[noun]] names a person, place or thing: “shepherd”, “Jerusalem”, “love”;
- a [[pronoun]] stands in for a noun: “he”, “them”, “who”;
- a [[verb]] says what happens or what someone does: “runs”, “was”, “will save”;
- an [[adjective]] describes a noun: “good shepherd”;
- an [[adverb]] describes how, when or where: “quickly”, “then”, “there”;
- an [[article|article]] is the word “the” (or “a”);
- a [[preposition]] shows how one thing relates to another: “in”, “from”, “with”;
- a [[conjunction]] joins words or sentences: “and”, “but”, “because”;
- a [[particle]] is a small word that adds a shade of meaning, such as “not”;
- an [[interjection]] is an exclamation: “Oh!”, “Look!”.

## noun | Noun
lang: both
aka: nouns, naming word, n., substantive
match: noun
see: proper-noun, case, gender, number, construct-state
find: grc ^N-[NGDAV][SP][MFN]$
find: he (^|/)Nc..a$
short: A word that names a person, place, thing or idea: “shepherd”, “city”, “love”.

Nouns are naming words. Anything you can put “the” in front of is probably a noun: the shepherd, the city, the word, the love of God. Names of particular people and places, such as Peter or Galilee, are nouns too: see [[proper-noun]].

In English a noun changes little: “word” becomes “words” for more than one. In Greek a noun also changes its ending to show its job in the sentence (its [[case]]). In Hebrew a noun can take a prefix such as “and”, “the” or “in”, and an ending such as “my” or “their”, all written as one word.

Every Greek and Hebrew noun also has a [[gender]], and is either one or more than one (its [[number]]).

## proper-noun | Names: proper nouns
lang: both
aka: proper noun, name, names, person's name, place name, name of a person, name of a place, title, people group, individual, location
match: proper noun; name=a name; of a type=a describing word used as a name or title, such as the Beautiful Gate; name of a person=the name of a particular person; name of a place=the name of a particular place; title=a title or a name used like one; people group=a word for a people, such as Galileans; individual=the name of a particular person; location=the name of a particular place
see: noun, gentilic, transcribed
find: grc ^N-...-P n=2
find: he (^|/)Np n=2
short: The name of a particular person, place or thing, such as Jesus, Moses or Jerusalem.

A proper noun is a name. In English we give names a capital letter: Peter, Galilee, Passover. Greek Bibles also start names with a capital letter. Hebrew has no capital letters, so the grammar label tells you instead.

STEPBible's labels sort names into kinds, and the app shows them in brackets: the name of a person, the name of a place, a title (such as “Christ” or a month's name), or a people group (such as “Galilean”, see [[gentilic]]).

Many names in the New Testament are Hebrew or Aramaic names written in Greek letters, and some of these never change their ending: see [[indeclinable]] and [[transcribed]].

## pronoun | Pronoun
lang: both
aka: pronouns, personal pronoun, pron.
match: pronoun; personal pronoun; personal
see: person, personal-pronoun, hebrew-pronouns, relative, demonstrative, interrogative, pronoun-suffix
find: grc ^P- n=2
find: he (^|/)Pp n=2
short: A word that stands in for a noun: “he”, “she”, “it”, “we”, “they”, “who”, “this”.

Instead of saying “Peter took Peter's boat”, we say “Peter took *his* boat”. Words like “I”, “you”, “he”, “his”, “them” and “who” are pronouns.

Greek has separate pronoun words, as English does, and they change their ending like nouns: see [[personal-pronoun]]. Hebrew has separate pronoun words for “I”, “you”, “he” and so on, but more often it adds a short ending to a word instead: “his word” is one word, דְּבָרוֹ. See [[pronoun-suffix]].

Pronouns come in several kinds, each with its own entry: personal (“he”), [[possessive]] (“my”), [[reflexive]] (“himself”), [[reciprocal]] (“one another”), [[demonstrative]] (“this”, “that”), [[relative]] (“who”, “which”), [[interrogative]] (“who?”) and [[indefinite]] (“someone”).

## verb | Verb
lang: both
aka: verbs, doing word, action word, v.
match: verb
see: tense, aspect, voice, mood, person, greek-verb, hebrew-verb
find: grc ^V- n=2
find: he (^|/)V n=2
short: A word for what happens or what someone does or is: “runs”, “said”, “will save”, “is”.

Verbs are the doing and being words. In “Jesus wept”, the verb is “wept”. Every complete sentence has one, though Hebrew and Greek can leave out “is” where English needs it: see [[verbless-clause]].

English shows the time of an action mostly with helper words: “I *will* go”, “I *was* going”. Greek and Hebrew show most of this, and who is doing it, inside the verb itself, with endings, prefixes and changes of vowel. One Greek or Hebrew verb can therefore be a whole English sentence: ἐδάκρυσεν, “he wept”.

A verb's grammar label describes it with several words: see [[tense]], [[aspect]], [[voice]], [[mood]], [[person]] and [[number]]. How verbs are built is explained in [[greek-verb]] and [[hebrew-verb]].

## adjective | Adjective
lang: both
aka: adjectives, describing word, adj.
match: adjective
see: noun, agreement, comparative, greek-adjective
find: grc ^A-[NGDAV] n=2
find: he (^|/)Aa n=2
short: A word that describes a noun: “good”, “holy”, “first”.
lessons: he-10

In “the good shepherd”, “good” is an adjective: it tells you what the shepherd is like. In English an adjective never changes. In Greek and Hebrew it changes to match the noun it describes, in [[gender]] and [[number]] (and in Greek in [[case]] too). This matching is called [[agreement]].

In both languages an adjective can also be used on its own as a noun, the way English says “the poor” or “the holy”: ὁ ἅγιος, “the holy one”.

## adverb | Adverb
lang: both
aka: adverbs, adv.
match: adverb; adverb or adverb and particle combined=an adverb with a small word such as “not” built into it; combined=two words joined into one
see: adjective, verb, negative
find: grc ^ADV$ n=2
find: he (^|/)D$ n=2
short: A word that says how, when, where or how much: “quickly”, “now”, “there”, “very”.

An adverb describes a verb, an adjective or a whole sentence, rather than a noun. In “he went out immediately”, “immediately” is an adverb. Many Greek adverbs end in -ως, as many English ones end in “-ly”: οὕτως, “in this way”. Adverbs don't change their form.

## article | The article, “the”
lang: both
aka: definite article, the, art., article
match: definite article; article
see: greek-article, hebrew-article, noun
find: grc ^T- n=2
find: he (^|/)Td n=2
short: The word “the”. Greek and Hebrew have “the” but no word for “a”.

English has two articles: “the” (the definite article) and “a” or “an” (the indefinite article). Greek and Hebrew have only “the”. Where there is no “the”, [[translations|English translations]] often add “a”: λόγος can be “a word”.

The two languages use “the” differently from English, and from each other. In Greek it is a separate word that changes its ending to match its noun, and it is used far more than English uses “the”, even before names: see [[greek-article]]. In Hebrew it is a prefix, הַ, joined to the front of the word: see [[hebrew-article]].

## preposition | Preposition
lang: both
aka: prepositions, prep.
match: preposition
see: case, prefixes, hebrew-prepositions
find: grc ^PREP$ n=2
find: he (^|/)R$ n=2
short: A small word that shows how one thing relates to another: “in”, “from”, “with”, “to”, “under”.

In “the Word was with God”, “with” is a preposition: it shows how the Word relates to God. A preposition goes before a noun or pronoun.

In Greek a preposition is a separate word, and the noun after it takes a particular [[case]]. Some Greek prepositions change their meaning with the case: μετά with one case means “with”, with another “after”. In Hebrew three common prepositions are one letter long and are joined to the front of the next word: see [[hebrew-prepositions]].

## conjunction | Conjunction
lang: both
aka: conjunctions, conj., joining word
match: conjunction
see: conjunction-vav, particle, wayyiqtol
find: grc ^CONJ$ n=2
find: he (^|/)C/ n=2
short: A word that joins words or sentences together: “and”, “but”, “for”, “because”, “if”.

Conjunctions are the joints of a sentence. καί, “and”, is the commonest word in the New Testament after “the”. Hebrew joins almost every sentence of a story with “and”, written as a one-letter prefix: see [[conjunction-vav]].

[[translations|English translations]] often leave these “and”s out or turn them into “then”, “so” or “but”, because English doesn't like starting every sentence with “and”.

## particle | Particle
lang: both
aka: particles, part., small word, disjunctive particle
match: particle; disjunctive=offering a choice, such as “or”
see: negative, interrogative-particle, conjunction
find: grc ^PRT n=2
short: A small word that doesn't change its form and adds a shade of meaning: “not”, “indeed”, “then”.

Particle is a catch-all name for small words that are not nouns, verbs or adjectives and never change their form. Greek has many: some make a sentence negative (οὐ, “not”), some ask a question, and some show how a sentence connects to the one before, often in ways English can only show with its tone of voice. Hebrew labels use “particle” for words such as “not” ([[negative]]), the question marker ([[interrogative-particle]]) and “this” ([[demonstrative-particle]]).

## interjection | Interjection
lang: both
aka: exclamation, interj., inj.
match: interjection; exclamation=an exclamation
see: particle, hinneh
find: grc ^INJ n=2
short: A word that is an exclamation on its own: “Oh!”, “Alas!”, “Look!”.

An interjection stands outside the grammar of the sentence, like a cry. In the New Testament, οὐαί is “woe!” or “how terrible!”, and ἰδού is “look!” or “see!”. Hebrew has הוֹי, “woe!”, and the very common [[hinneh|הִנֵּה, “look!”]], among others.

## subject | Subject
lang: both
aka: subjects, doer, who does it
see: object, nominative, person, verb
short: The person or thing a sentence is about: usually the one doing the action. In “Jesus wept”, Jesus is the subject.

Ask “who or what is doing this?” and the answer is the subject. In “the shepherd calls the sheep”, the shepherd is the subject.

In English the subject usually comes before the verb. In Greek you can tell the subject from its ending, the [[nominative]] case, wherever it stands. In both Greek and Hebrew the verb itself shows who the subject is ([[person]] and [[number]]), so a separate word for “he” or “they” is often left out: λέγει means “he says”.

## object | Object
lang: both
aka: direct object, indirect object, objects, done to, receiver
see: subject, accusative, dative, object-marker
short: The person or thing an action is done to. In “the shepherd calls the sheep”, the sheep are the object.

Ask “whom or what?” after the verb: “calls whom? the sheep”. That is the direct object. Some verbs also have an indirect object, the person something is given to or done for: in “he gave *them* bread”, “them” is the indirect object.

In Greek the direct object usually has the [[accusative]] ending and the indirect object the [[dative]]. In Hebrew a definite direct object often has the small word אֵת in front of it: see [[object-marker]].

## sentence-parts | Sentences, clauses and phrases
aka: sentence, clause, phrase, main clause
see: subject, verb, conjunction, verbless-clause
short: A sentence is a complete thought; a clause is a part of a sentence with its own verb; a phrase is a group of words without one.

“When the Sabbath was over, they bought spices” is one sentence with two clauses: “when the Sabbath was over” and “they bought spices”. Each clause has its own [[verb]]. A phrase is a group of words that belongs together but has no verb of its own: “in the beginning”, “the word of God”.

Grammar books talk about clauses because the [[mood]] or form of a verb often depends on what kind of clause it is in, such as a clause beginning “so that” or “if”.

## person | Person: 1st, 2nd and 3rd
lang: both
aka: 1st person, 2nd person, 3rd person, first person, second person, third person, persons
match: person=who is involved: speaker, spoken to, or spoken about; 1st=the one speaking or writing: I, we; 2nd=the one spoken to: you; 3rd=someone or something being talked about: he, she, it, they; first=the one speaking or writing: I, we; second=the one spoken to: you; third=someone or something being talked about: he, she, it, they
see: number, subject, verb, pronoun
short: Who is involved: 1st person is the speaker (I, we), 2nd person is the one spoken to (you), 3rd person is anyone else (he, she, it, they).
lessons: grc-4

Grammar counts three “persons”:

- 1st person: the one speaking or writing, “I” or (with others) “we”;
- 2nd person: the one being spoken to, “you”;
- 3rd person: anyone or anything else, “he”, “she”, “it” or “they”.

Greek and Hebrew verbs show the person with their ending or prefix, so “I” or “they” is usually not written as a separate word. A label such as “3rd singular” means “he, she or it”; “2nd plural” means “you”, speaking to more than one person. Hebrew labels write the words out, “third singular”, and under each word the app shortens it further: see [[pgn]].

English “you” is the same for one person or many. Greek and Hebrew are not, so the label tells you which: the app writes “you (one)” and “you (more than one)”, or older English Bibles' “thou” and “ye”.

## number | Number: singular, plural and dual
lang: both
aka: singular, plural, dual, sg., pl., du., numbers
match: singular=one; plural=more than one; dual=two, a pair
see: person, gender, agreement, dual
short: Whether a word means one (singular) or more than one (plural). Hebrew also has a form for two (dual).
lessons: grc-4, he-6

English shows number with endings such as “word, words” or “child, children”. Greek and Hebrew do the same, with their own endings: λόγος, “word”, and λόγοι, “words”; דָּבָר, “word”, and דְּבָרִים, “words”.

A verb also has a number, to match its [[subject]]: “he says” is singular, “they say” is plural.

Hebrew has a third number, the dual, for things that come in twos, especially pairs of body parts: יָדַיִם, “two hands”. See [[dual]].

## gender | Gender: masculine, feminine and neuter
lang: both
aka: masculine, feminine, neuter, masc., fem., neut., common gender, male or female, either gender, m, f
match: masculine=grammatically masculine; feminine=grammatically feminine; neuter=grammatically neither masculine nor feminine; male or female=the same form for either gender; common=the same form for either gender
see: agreement, noun, number
short: Every Greek and Hebrew noun is grammatically masculine or feminine (and in Greek it can also be neuter). It is a grammar class, not a statement about the thing itself.
lessons: grc-6, he-6

In English only people and animals have a gender (“he”, “she”), and things are “it”. In Greek and Hebrew every noun belongs to a gender class, even words for things and ideas. Greek has three: masculine, feminine and neuter (meaning “neither”). Hebrew has two: masculine and feminine.

Grammatical gender is mostly about word shapes, not about meaning: Greek λόγος, “word”, is masculine; ἀλήθεια, “truth”, is feminine; and τέκνον, “child”, is neuter. For people, though, the gender usually matches.

Gender matters because words that go together must match: an [[adjective]], the [[article|article]] “the” and a pronoun take the same gender as their noun. Verbs show gender too in Hebrew: “you said” is a different word when speaking to a man or to a woman. Where one form serves for both, Hebrew labels say “either gender”, which the app writes as “male or female”; grammar books call it “common” (c in 3cp).

## agreement | Agreement: words that match
aka: agree, concord, matching
see: gender, number, case, adjective
short: Words that belong together take matching forms: an adjective matches its noun, and a verb matches its subject.
lessons: he-10

In English we say “he walks” but “they walk”: the verb agrees with its subject. Greek and Hebrew do much more of this. An adjective and “the” take the same [[gender]], [[number]] and (in Greek) [[case]] as the noun they belong with: ὁ ποιμὴν ὁ καλός, “the good shepherd”, has three masculine, singular, [[nominative]] words.

Agreement helps you read: it shows which words belong together, even when they are far apart in the sentence.

## inflection | Endings and inflection
aka: inflection, inflected, ending, endings, suffix, prefix, form, forms, paradigm, conjugation, declension
see: stem, dictionary-form, declension, greek-verb, hebrew-verb
short: Changing a word's shape (usually its ending) to show its grammar, as English “walk” becomes “walked” or “walks”.

English does this a little: “walk, walks, walked, walking”, or “he, him, his”. This changing of a word's shape is called inflection. Greek and Hebrew do it much more. A Greek noun changes its ending to show its [[case]] and number; a Greek verb changes its ending to show who is doing it and when. A Hebrew word adds prefixes and endings, and its vowels shift as it grows.

A table showing all the forms of a word is called a paradigm. Learning the common patterns, rather than every form of every word, is how most of the grammar is learned: the lessons give the tables you need, one at a time.

The part of the word that carries its meaning is the [[stem]]. What is added to it is a prefix (at the front) or an ending, also called a suffix (at the back).

## stem | Stem
aka: stem, base, verb stem
see: root, inflection, dictionary-form
unattested: λύεις, λύομεν
short: The part of a word that carries its meaning, to which endings are added: λυ- in λύω, λύεις, λύομεν.

Take away the ending from a word, and what is left is its stem. In λόγος, “word”, the stem is λογ- and the ending is -ος. Different endings on the same stem give different forms: λόγου, “of a word”, λόγῳ, “to a word”.

In Hebrew grammar the word “stem” is also used for something else: the seven or so patterns a Hebrew verb can follow, such as [[qal]] or [[piel]]. See [[stems]]. The three consonants underneath a Hebrew word are called its [[root]].

## tense | Tense: time
aka: tense, tenses, time, past, present, future
match: past=in the past; present=in the present; future=still to come
see: aspect, present, aorist, future, perfect-greek, perfect-hebrew, imperfect-hebrew
short: The form of a verb that shows when something happens: in the past, the present or the future.

In English, “I go”, “I went” and “I will go” differ in tense. Greek grammar uses the word tense for each set of verb forms ([[present]], [[imperfect-greek|imperfect]], [[future]], [[aorist]], [[perfect-greek|perfect]], [[pluperfect]]), but these forms show more than time: they also show how the action is viewed, which is called [[aspect]].

Hebrew verbs are even less about time. Their two main forms (see [[perfect-hebrew]] and [[imperfect-hebrew]]) mostly show whether an action is seen as complete or not. The time comes from the context. That is why STEPBible's Hebrew labels say “past/present” or “future/present”: either may be right.

## aspect | Aspect: how an action is viewed
aka: verbal aspect, perfective, imperfective, complete, incomplete, ongoing
see: tense, aorist, present, imperfect-greek, perfect-hebrew, imperfect-hebrew
short: Whether an action is seen as a whole event or as something going on. “He walked to town” views it whole; “he was walking to town” views it in progress.

English can say the same event two ways. “Jesus wept” presents the weeping as a single fact. “Jesus was weeping” takes us inside it while it goes on. That difference, between viewing an action from outside as a whole and from inside as it unfolds, is called aspect.

Greek shows aspect in its verb forms. The [[aorist]] presents an action as a whole; the [[present]] and [[imperfect-greek|imperfect]] present it as ongoing or repeated; the [[perfect-greek|perfect]] presents a finished action whose result still stands. Hebrew's main verb forms are also mostly about aspect: complete ([[perfect-hebrew]]) or not complete ([[imperfect-hebrew]]).

Scholars still discuss exactly how much Greek and Hebrew verb forms say about time and how much about aspect. For reading, the safe rule is: let the context decide the time, and let the form tell you how the writer views the action.

## voice | Voice: active, middle and passive
aka: voice, voices
see: active, passive, middle, middle-or-passive, deponent, stems
short: Whether the subject does the action (active: “he taught”), receives it (passive: “he was taught”), or acts for or on itself (middle).

“The shepherd *calls* the sheep” is active: the subject does the action. “The sheep *are called* by the shepherd” is passive: the subject receives it. Voice is the name for this difference.

Greek has a third voice, the [[middle]], in which the subject is involved in the action in some special way, often doing it for itself. In Hebrew, active and passive (and reflexive, “doing it to oneself”) are part of the verb's [[stems|stem]] rather than a separate voice.

## active | Active
lang: both
aka: active voice, act.
match: active=the subject does the action
see: voice, passive, middle
find: grc ^V-[2]?[PIFARL]A n=2
short: The subject does the action: “he taught”, “they see”.

Most verbs, most of the time, are active. In “God loved the world”, God does the loving. In Hebrew labels, “active” is part of the [[stems|stem]] name: [[qal]] is “simple, active”, [[hiphil]] “causative, active”. Its partner is the [[passive]].

## passive | Passive
lang: both
aka: passive voice, pass.
match: passive=the subject receives the action
see: voice, active, middle-or-passive, niphal, pual, hophal
find: grc ^V-[2]?[PIFARL]P n=2
short: The subject receives the action instead of doing it: “he was taught”, “it is written”.

In “it is written”, nobody is named as the writer: the passive puts the thing written in the place of the subject. In English we build the passive with “be” and a verb form: “was taken”, “is written”, “will be saved”. Greek has passive endings. Hebrew has passive [[stems]]: the [[niphal]], [[pual]] and [[hophal]].

Sometimes a passive with no one named hides a reference to God: “they will be comforted” ([MAT 5:4]) means God will comfort them. Grammar books call this the divine passive.

## mood | Mood
aka: mood, moods, mode
see: indicative, subjunctive, imperative, optative, infinitive, participle, jussive, cohortative
short: The form of a verb that shows how the speaker presents the action: as a fact, a possibility, a wish or a command.

“You go” states a fact. “Go!” is a command. “If you were to go” is a possibility. These differences are called mood.

Greek has four moods with endings that show a [[person]]: [[indicative]] (statements and questions), [[subjunctive]] (what may happen), [[optative]] (wishes) and [[imperative]] (commands). [[grammar-labels|Grammar labels]] also list the [[infinitive]] (“to go”) and the [[participle]] (“going”) in the same place, though they are not moods in the strict sense. Hebrew has forms for commands and wishes too: [[imperative]], [[jussive]] and [[cohortative]].

## indicative | Indicative
lang: both
aka: indicative mood, ind., statement
match: indicative=a statement of fact (or a question)
see: mood, subjunctive, imperative
find: grc ^V-[2]?[PIFARL][A-Z]I- n=2
short: The ordinary form of a verb for saying that something is, was or will be so: “he went”, “God is love”.

Most verbs in a story are indicative. They state facts, or ask about them: “did he go?”. In Greek, only the indicative shows time clearly: it is the only form that has an [[augment]] for the past.

STEPBible's Hebrew labels add “indicative” in brackets to show that a form is used as a plain statement, as opposed to a wish ([[jussive]], [[cohortative]]).

## imperative | Imperative: commands
lang: both
aka: command, imperative mood, impv., impv
match: imperative=a command or request
see: mood, jussive, cohortative, prohibition
find: grc ^V-[2]?[PIFARL][A-Z]M- n=2
find: he (^|/)V[a-zA-Z]v n=2
short: The form of a verb for telling someone to do something: “Come!”, “Listen!”, “Love your neighbour.”

A command speaks to someone, so it is usually [[person|2nd person]]: “you, do this!”. Greek also has a 3rd person imperative, which English translates “let him…” or “let it…”: γενηθήτω τὸ θέλημά σου, “let your will be done”.

In Greek the [[aspect]] still matters in a command. An [[aorist]] imperative tells someone to do a thing; a [[present]] imperative often tells them to keep doing it, or to make it their habit.

Hebrew imperatives are always 2nd person. Hebrew commands for “let him” or “let us” use the [[jussive]] and [[cohortative]]. A command not to do something is not made with the imperative in either language: see [[prohibition]].

## prohibition | Commands not to do something
aka: prohibition, negative command, do not, don't
see: imperative, jussive, subjunctive, negative
short: “Do not…”. Greek uses μή with an [[imperative]] or [[subjunctive]]; Hebrew uses לֹא or אַל with the [[imperfect-hebrew|imperfect]].

In Greek, “do not” is μή, never οὐ. With a [[present]] imperative it often means “stop doing” or “don't make a habit of it”: μὴ φοβοῦ, “do not be afraid”. With an [[aorist]] [[subjunctive]] it means “don't start”: μὴ φοβηθῇς.

In Hebrew, לֹא with the [[imperfect-hebrew|imperfect]] gives a lasting command: לֹא תִּרְצָח, “you shall not murder”. אַל with the [[jussive]] gives a more immediate “don't”.

## infinitive | Infinitive
lang: both
aka: infinitive, inf., to-form, verbal noun
match: infinitive=the “to …” form of a verb
see: participle, greek-infinitive, infinitive-construct, infinitive-absolute
find: grc ^V-[2]?[PIFARL][A-Z]N n=2
short: The form of a verb that names the action itself, without a person: “to go”, “to love”, “going”.

“I want *to go*” and “*To love* is to give” use infinitives. An infinitive has no [[person]] or [[number]]: it names the action, a little like a noun.

[[greek-infinitive|Greek infinitives]] have their own endings, such as -ειν or -σαι, and can even take “the” in front of them. Hebrew has two infinitives with different uses: the [[infinitive-construct]], used like “to go” or “when he went”, and the [[infinitive-absolute]], often used to strengthen another verb.

## participle | Participle
lang: both
aka: participle, ptc., ptcp., -ing form, verbal adjective
match: participle=a verb used like an adjective or a noun: “going”, “one who goes”
see: greek-participle, hebrew-participle, infinitive, adjective
find: grc ^V-[2]?[PIFARL][A-Z]P- n=2
find: he (^|/)V[a-zA-Z][rs] n=2
short: A verb used as an adjective or noun: “the *sleeping* child”, “the one *who believes*”, “*having said* this”.

English participles end in “-ing” or “-ed”: “a *burning* bush”, “the *written* word”. They are part verb (they have a [[tense]] and a [[voice]]) and part adjective (they describe a noun).

Greek uses participles constantly, much more than English, and they have the endings of an [[adjective]]. English often has to turn them into a whole clause: ὁ πιστεύων, literally “the believing”, is “the one who believes”. See [[greek-participle]]. Hebrew uses participles for ongoing actions and for people who do something: see [[hebrew-participle]].

## comparative | Comparative and superlative
lang: both
aka: comparative, superlative, comp., more, most, -er, -est
match: comparative=“more …”, or “-er”; superlative=“most …”, or “-est”
see: adjective, adverb
short: The forms that compare: “greater” is comparative, “greatest” is superlative.

English adds “-er” and “-est”, or “more” and “most”: great, greater, greatest. Greek has endings for the same: μείζων, “greater”, from μέγας, “great”. In the New Testament the comparative often does the work of the superlative too, so μείζων can mean “greatest” ([MAT 18:1]).

Hebrew has no separate forms for this. It says “greater than” with the preposition מִן, “from”: “good *from* him” means “better than him”.

## numbers-words | Numbers
lang: both
aka: number words, numeral, numerals, cardinal, ordinal, numerical, numerical position, first, second, two, three
match: numeral=a number word; numerical=a number word (one, two, three); numerical position=a number for position in order (first, second, third)
see: adjective, indeclinable, dual
find: he (^|/)A[co] n=2
short: Words for numbers. Cardinal numbers count (one, two, three); ordinal numbers put things in order (first, second, third).

Number words are a kind of [[adjective]]. In Greek, one to four change their endings; most larger numbers never change ([[indeclinable]]). In Hebrew, numbers behave in surprising ways: from three to ten, a masculine noun takes a number word with a feminine-looking ending, and the other way round.

STEPBible's Hebrew labels call counting numbers “numerical” and order numbers “numerical position”. The app writes them as “number” and “number (order)”.

## negative | Not: negative words
lang: both
aka: not, negation, negative particle, no, neg.
match: negative=a “not” word, or a word with “not” built in (no one, never); not=the word “not”
see: prohibition, particle, adverb
find: grc ^PRT-N n=2
find: he (^|/)Tn n=2
short: Words for “not”, “no” and “never”. Greek has two, οὐ and μή; Hebrew has לֹא and אַל among others.

Greek has two ways of saying “not”. οὐ (οὐκ before a vowel, οὐχ before a rough breathing) denies a fact: “he is not here”. μή is used with wishes, commands, conditions and most forms that are not the [[indicative]]: “do not be afraid”. A question that begins with οὐ expects the answer “yes”; one that begins with μή expects “no”.

Hebrew uses לֹא for most statements and lasting commands, and אַל for urgent ones (see [[prohibition]]). Other words such as אֵין, “there is not”, and בְּלִי, “without”, also say “not”.

Some Greek words have the “not” built in, such as οὐδείς, “no one”, and μηδέ, “and not”. STEPBible's labels mark these “negative” too.
