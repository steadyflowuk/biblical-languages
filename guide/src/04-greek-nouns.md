# greek-nouns | Greek nouns, “the” and pronouns

Greek nouns, adjectives, pronouns and the word “the” change their endings to show what job they do in the sentence. This section explains those endings, called cases, the three families of nouns, and the different kinds of pronoun.

## case | Case: what a noun's ending shows
lang: grc
aka: cases, case ending, case endings
see: nominative, genitive, dative, accusative, vocative, declension
lessons: grc-6, grc-7
unattested: λόγε
short: The case is the form of a Greek noun (and of “the”, adjectives and pronouns) that shows its job in the sentence: subject, object, “of”, “to” and so on.

English shows a noun's job mostly by word order. “The dog bit the man” and “the man bit the dog” have the same words in a different order. Greek shows the job with the ending instead, so the words can come in almost any order.

English still has a little of this in its pronouns: “he” is the subject (he saw her), “him” is the object (she saw him), and “his” means “of him”. Greek does the same with every noun.

Greek has five cases:

- [[nominative]]: the subject, “*the shepherd* calls”;
- [[genitive]]: “of”, “*the shepherd's* voice”;
- [[dative]]: “to” or “for”, “he gave it *to the shepherd*”;
- [[accusative]]: the object, “they saw *the shepherd*”;
- [[vocative]]: calling someone, “*Shepherd*, come!”.

The endings for each case depend on the noun's [[gender]], its [[number]], and which family of nouns it belongs to (its [[declension]]).

Table: λόγος, “word”, in each case
| case | one | more than one |
| nominative | λόγος | λόγοι |
| genitive | λόγου | λόγων |
| dative | λόγῳ | λόγοις |
| accusative | λόγον | λόγους |
| vocative | λόγε | λόγοι |

## nominative | Nominative
lang: grc
aka: nom., nominative case, subject case
match: nominative=the subject case
see: case, subject, dictionary-form
lessons: grc-6
find: grc ^N-N..$
short: The case of the subject, the person or thing doing the action: “*the Word* was”. Dictionaries list nouns in the nominative.

In [JHN 1:1], ὁ λόγος, “the Word”, is nominative, because it is the [[subject]]: the Word was. The nominative is also used after “is” or “was” for what the subject is: θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος, “the Word was God”. Here both θεός and λόγος are nominative (θεός is written θεὸς before another word: see [[accents]]), and the [[greek-article|word “the”]] shows which is the subject.

Names and titles in lists, headings and greetings are often in the nominative too: Παῦλος, “Paul”, at the start of a letter.

## genitive | Genitive
lang: grc
aka: gen., genitive case, possessive case, of
match: genitive=the “of” case
see: case, dative, genitive-absolute
lessons: grc-7
find: grc ^N-G..$
short: The “of” case: it shows whose something is or what it is connected with: “the word *of God*”, “*Jesus'* disciples”.

The genitive usually describes or narrows down another noun, and English translates it with “of” or with “'s”: ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ, “the word of God”. It can show belonging (the house of Peter), source (the gospel of God), what something is made of, or what kind of thing it is (the body of sin: a sinful body).

The genitive is also used after some [[preposition|prepositions]], such as ἐκ, “out of”, ἀπό, “from”, and διά, “through”, and to compare (“greater than him”).

Greek grammars name many kinds of genitive. The context, not the ending, decides which one fits: “the love of God” can be God's love for us or our love for God.

## dative | Dative
lang: grc
aka: dat., dative case, to, for, indirect object
match: dative=the “to” or “for” case
see: case, object, accusative
lessons: grc-7
find: grc ^N-D..$
short: The “to” or “for” case: the person something is given or said to, and also “with” or “by”: “he said *to them*”.

The dative marks the indirect [[object]]: εἶπεν αὐτοῖς, “he said to them”. It is also used for the means by which something is done (“by grace”, “with water”), for place (“in the house”) and time (“on the third day”).

Many prepositions take the dative, especially ἐν, “in”, and σύν, “with”. Some verbs take their object in the dative instead of the [[accusative]], such as πιστεύω, “I believe (in)”, and ἀκολουθέω, “I follow”.

## accusative | Accusative
lang: grc
aka: acc., accusative case, object case, direct object
match: accusative=the object case
see: case, object, nominative
lessons: grc-6
find: grc ^N-A..$
short: The case of the direct object, the person or thing the action is done to: “God loved *the world*”.

In “God loved the world”, the world is what was loved, so in Greek it is accusative: τὸν κόσμον. The accusative also follows some prepositions, especially εἰς, “into”, and πρός, “to” or “with”, and it can show how long something lasts (“for three days”).

Neuter nouns use the same form for the [[nominative]] and accusative, so the context has to show which is meant.

## vocative | Vocative
lang: grc
aka: voc., vocative case, calling, address, O
match: vocative=the case for calling someone
see: case, nominative
find: grc ^N-V
short: The case for calling or speaking to someone: “*Lord*, teach us to pray”.

When someone is spoken to directly, their name or title is in the vocative: κύριε, “Lord!”, πάτερ, “Father!”. Older English used “O”: “O Lord”. Many nouns have no separate vocative and use the [[nominative]] form instead, sometimes with “the”: ὁ πατήρ, “Father!”.

## declension | Declensions: the three noun families
lang: grc
aka: declension, first declension, second declension, third declension, 1st declension, 2nd declension, 3rd declension, decline, declined
see: case, first-declension, second-declension, third-declension, inflection
lessons: grc-6, grc-9
short: Greek nouns fall into three families, each with its own set of case endings. The family is called a declension.

All Greek nouns use the same five [[case|cases]], but not the same endings. There are three sets of endings, and every noun follows one of them:

- [[first-declension]]: mostly feminine nouns ending in -α or -η, such as ἡμέρα, “day”, and γραφή, “scripture”;
- [[second-declension]]: mostly masculine nouns ending in -ος, such as λόγος, and neuter nouns in -ον, such as τέκνον, “child”;
- [[third-declension]]: everything else, such as σάρξ, “flesh”, πατήρ, “father”, and ὄνομα, “name”.

[[grammar-labels|Grammar labels]] don't say which family a noun belongs to: it doesn't change the meaning. But knowing the families lets you recognise a case ending you have never seen on that word. Dictionaries help by giving the [[genitive]] ending after the noun: λόγος, -ου; σάρξ, σαρκός.

## first-declension | First declension
lang: grc
aka: 1st declension, first declension nouns, α-declension, alpha declension
see: declension, second-declension, third-declension
lessons: grc-9
short: The family of nouns that end in -α or -η, mostly feminine: ἡμέρα, γραφή, δόξα. A few masculine ones end in -ης or -ας.

Most first declension nouns are feminine, and end in -η or -α in the dictionary: ἀγάπη, “love”, καρδία, “heart”, δόξα, “glory”. Their endings have η or α in the singular and are the same in the plural for all of them: -αι, -ων, -αις, -ας.

A small group are masculine and end in -ης or -ας: προφήτης, “prophet”, μαθητής, “disciple”. They use the same plural.

| case | one | more than one |
| nominative | γραφή | γραφαί |
| genitive | γραφῆς | γραφῶν |
| dative | γραφῇ | γραφαῖς |
| accusative | γραφήν | γραφάς |

## second-declension | Second declension
lang: grc
aka: 2nd declension, second declension nouns, o-declension, omicron declension
see: declension, first-declension, third-declension, neuter
lessons: grc-6, grc-8
short: The family of masculine nouns in -ος, such as λόγος, and neuter nouns in -ον, such as ἔργον, “work”.

This is the family most learners meet first. Most nouns ending in -ος are masculine, but a few are feminine, such as ὁδός, “road”, and ἔρημος, “desert”. The article shows which: ἡ ὁδός.

Neuter nouns end in -ον, and their plural ends in -α: ἔργον, “work”, ἔργα, “works”. For neuter nouns the [[nominative]] and [[accusative]] are always the same.

Table: ἔργον, “work”
| case | one | more than one |
| nominative and accusative | ἔργον | ἔργα |
| genitive | ἔργου | ἔργων |
| dative | ἔργῳ | ἔργοις |

## third-declension | Third declension
lang: grc
aka: 3rd declension, third declension nouns, consonant declension
see: declension, first-declension, second-declension
short: The family of all other nouns, whose stem often only shows in the other cases: σάρξ, “flesh”, σαρκός, “of flesh”.

Third declension nouns have many different endings in the [[nominative]] singular, which makes them harder to recognise: σάρξ, πατήρ, ὄνομα, πόλις, πνεῦμα. Their real [[stem]] shows in the other cases: σαρκ-, πατρ-, ὀνοματ-.

That is why dictionaries give the [[genitive]] with every noun. The genitive singular of these nouns usually ends in -ος: σαρκός, πατρός, ὀνόματος. The other endings are fairly regular: the [[dative]] singular ends in -ι, the [[accusative]] in -α or -ν, and the dative plural in -σι (or -σιν: see [[movable-nu]]).

Table: σάρξ, “flesh”, and ὄνομα, “name”
| case | σάρξ | ὄνομα |
| nominative | σάρξ | ὄνομα |
| genitive | σαρκός | ὀνόματος |
| dative | σαρκί | ὀνόματι |
| accusative | σάρκα | ὄνομα |

## neuter | Neuter
lang: grc
aka: neuter gender, neut., neither
see: gender, second-declension
short: The third Greek gender, meaning “neither” masculine nor feminine. Neuter nouns use the same form for subject and object.
lessons: grc-8

Neuter nouns include many things and ideas (ἔργον, “work”, πνεῦμα, “spirit”), but also τέκνον and παιδίον, “child”, because grammatical [[gender]] is about word shape, not meaning.

Two rules help with neuter words. Their [[nominative]] and [[accusative]] forms are always the same. And a neuter plural subject often takes a singular verb: τὰ πρόβατα… ἀκούει, “the sheep hear” ([JHN 10:3]).

## greek-article | The Greek article: ὁ, ἡ, τό
lang: grc
aka: the, ho, article forms, definite article, ὁ ἡ τό
see: article, case, gender, agreement
lessons: grc-7, grc-9
find: grc ^T-
short: Greek's word for “the”. It changes its ending to match its noun in gender, number and case, and it is used more often than English “the”.

The article has the same ending patterns as nouns of the first two families, so knowing it helps you read the ending of the noun after it. It has no accent in four of its forms (ὁ, ἡ, οἱ, αἱ), and starts with τ in all the others.

Table: “the” in every form
| case | masculine | feminine | neuter |
| nominative singular | ὁ | ἡ | τό |
| genitive singular | τοῦ | τῆς | τοῦ |
| dative singular | τῷ | τῇ | τῷ |
| accusative singular | τόν | τήν | τό |
| nominative plural | οἱ | αἱ | τά |
| genitive plural | τῶν | τῶν | τῶν |
| dative plural | τοῖς | ταῖς | τοῖς |
| accusative plural | τούς | τάς | τά |

Greek uses the article in places where English doesn't. It often comes before names (ὁ Ἰησοῦς, “Jesus”) and before words for ideas (ἡ ἀγάπη, “love”), and English leaves it out. Where Greek has no article, English sometimes adds “a” and sometimes nothing.

The article can also turn almost anything into a noun: τὰ ἄνω, “the things above”, ὁ πιστεύων, “the one who believes”. And it can stand for a person on its own: ὁ δέ, “and he”. Whether a noun has an article or not can matter for its meaning, and such places, like [JHN 1:1], are much discussed.

## greek-adjective | How Greek adjectives work
lang: grc
aka: adjective position, attributive, predicate, predicative, substantival, adjectives in Greek
see: adjective, agreement, greek-article, comparative
short: A Greek adjective takes the same gender, number and case as its noun. Where it stands next to “the” decides whether it means “the good man” or “the man is good”.

A Greek adjective has endings like a noun's. It copies the [[gender]], [[number]] and [[case]] of the noun it describes, which is called [[agreement]].

Its place matters. When the adjective has the article “the” directly in front of it, it describes the noun: ὁ ἀγαθὸς ἄνθρωπος, or ὁ ἄνθρωπος ὁ ἀγαθός, both mean “the good man”. When the article is in front of the noun only, the adjective says something about it, as a statement: ὁ ἄνθρωπος ἀγαθός, “the man is good”.

An adjective with “the” and no noun works as a noun: ὁ ἀγαθός, “the good man”, or τὸ ἀγαθόν, “what is good”.

## indeclinable | Indeclinable
lang: grc
aka: indeclinable, indecl., does not change, unchanging
match: indeclinable=never changes its ending; letter=the name of a letter of the alphabet
see: transcribed, numbers-words, case
short: A word that never changes its ending, whatever its case: many Hebrew names, such as Ἀβραάμ, and most numbers.

Most Greek nouns change their ending to show their [[case]]. Some never do. They are called indeclinable (they can't be “declined”, meaning put through their cases). Many are names from the Old Testament, written in Greek letters: Ἀβραάμ, Ἰσραήλ, Ἰερουσαλήμ. Numbers above four are usually indeclinable too: δώδεκα, “twelve”.

With these words the case has to come from the rest of the sentence: from the article in front of it, a preposition, or the sense. The grammar label still gives the case, as worked out from the context.

The letters Alpha and Omega in [REV 1:8] are labelled “indeclinable letter”: they are the names of letters, used as nouns.

## transcribed | Transcribed words
lang: grc
aka: transcribed from Hebrew, transcribed from Aramaic, loanword, borrowed word, transliterated word
match: transcribed from hebrew=a Hebrew word written in Greek letters; transcribed from aramaic=an Aramaic word written in Greek letters; transcribed=written in Greek letters from another language
see: indeclinable, aramaic-language, proper-noun
find: grc -(HEB|ARAM)$
short: A Hebrew or Aramaic word kept as it was and written in Greek letters, such as ἀμήν, “amen”, or Ταλιθα, “little girl”.

Some words were taken into Greek without being translated, just as English Bibles keep “amen” and “hallelujah”. They are written in Greek letters but are really Hebrew or Aramaic: ἀμήν, “truly”; ὡσαννά, “hosanna”, from a Hebrew cry meaning “save, please”; ῥαββί, “my teacher”; πάσχα, “Passover”.

Most of them never change their ending ([[indeclinable]]). STEPBible's labels say “transcribed from Hebrew” or “transcribed from Aramaic”. The Aramaic words of Jesus are explained in [[aramaic-language]].

## contracted | Contracted form
lang: grc
aka: contracted form, contracted
match: contracted form=a form marked as shortened in the tagging; contracted=a form marked as shortened in the tagging
see: comparative, contract-verbs
count: elder grc ^N-.*-C$
short: A label STEPBible puts on a few words. In the New Testament it is used only for πρεσβύτερος, “elder”, which began as a word meaning “older”.

The tagging system marks some nouns as a “contracted form”. In the New Testament as the app has it, the {{elder}} words with this label are all forms of πρεσβύτερος, “elder”, as in the elders of the church or the elders of the people. The word is the [[comparative|comparative]] of an older word for “old man”, so it first meant “older”, and became a title. For reading, treat it as a normal noun of the [[second-declension]].

In the same tagging system, the ending -C on an adjective means comparative, and that may be what was meant here. Contraction in Greek usually means two vowels merging into one, as in the [[contract-verbs]].

## attic | Attic Greek form
lang: grc
aka: Attic, Attic form, classical Greek
match: attic greek form=an older spelling from the Greek of Athens; attic=an older spelling from the Greek of Athens
see: koine-greek, augment
short: A form spelt the older way, as in the classical Greek of Athens, rather than the usual Koine way.

Koine grew out of Attic, the Greek of Athens in its golden age. Most Attic forms had changed by the time of the New Testament, but a few writers kept some of the older ones. STEPBible marks such forms “Attic Greek form”. The example below, ἠδυνήθητε, “you were able”, has an η at the front where Koine more often wrote ε (see [[augment]]). The meaning is the same.

> ACT 13:38 ἠδυνήθητε

## personal-pronoun | Greek personal pronouns
lang: grc
aka: I, you, he, she, it, we, they, ἐγώ, σύ, αὐτός, personal pronouns in Greek
see: pronoun, person, case, reflexive
find: grc ^P-
short: The Greek words for “I”, “you”, “he”, “she”, “it”, “we” and “they”: ἐγώ, σύ, αὐτός, ἡμεῖς, ὑμεῖς. They change their ending for case like nouns.

Greek verbs already show who is doing the action, so a word for “I” or “you” is often left out. When a writer does use one, it is usually for emphasis or contrast: ἐγώ εἰμι ὁ ἄρτος τῆς ζωῆς, “*I* am the bread of life”.

The forms change with [[case]]: ἐγώ is “I”, με is “me”, μου is “of me” or “my”, μοι is “to me”. The commonest way to say “my” or “his” is the [[genitive]] of the pronoun after the noun: ὁ λόγος μου, “my word”, literally “the word of me”.

αὐτός does three jobs. On its own it means “he”, “she” or “it” (αὐτοῦ, “his”). Between “the” and its noun it means “same”: τὸ αὐτὸ πνεῦμα, “the same Spirit”. Outside them, with no “the” of its own, it means “himself”: αὐτὸς ὁ θεός, “God himself”.

## possessive | Possessive pronoun
lang: grc
aka: my, your, our, possessive adjective, ἐμός, σός, ἡμέτερος, ὑμέτερος
match: possessive=a word for “my”, “your”, “our”
see: personal-pronoun, genitive, pronoun
find: grc ^S- avoid=JHN15:27
short: Greek words for “my”, “your”, “our”, such as ἐμός and σός. They are rarer than the [[genitive]] of “I” or “you”.

The full set is ἐμός, σός, ἡμέτερος and ὑμέτερος. Most often, though, Greek says “my” with μου, the [[genitive]] of ἐγώ: ὁ πατήρ μου, “my father”. Sometimes it uses a separate word that works like an [[adjective]], and STEPBible's labels call it a possessive pronoun. It agrees with the thing owned, not with the owner: ἡ χαρὰ ἡ ἐμή, “my joy”, is feminine because χαρά is. These words are often a little emphatic: “*my* joy”.

## reflexive | Reflexive pronoun
lang: grc
aka: myself, yourself, himself, herself, themselves, ἑαυτοῦ, ἐμαυτοῦ, σεαυτοῦ
match: reflexive=a word for “-self”, pointing back to the subject
see: pronoun, middle, personal-pronoun
find: grc ^F- avoid=MRK9:16
short: A word for “myself”, “yourself”, “himself”: ἐμαυτοῦ, σεαυτοῦ, ἑαυτοῦ. It points back to the subject of its own clause.

In “he humbled himself”, “himself” is the same person as the subject. Greek uses a reflexive pronoun here: ἐταπείνωσεν ἑαυτόν ([PHP 2:8]). Reflexive pronouns have no [[nominative]], because they are never the subject.

The labels say “1st” for “myself”, “2nd” for “yourself”, “3rd” for “himself, herself, itself, themselves”. In the plural ἑαυτῶν can serve for all three persons: “ourselves”, “yourselves”, “themselves”.

## reciprocal | Reciprocal pronoun
lang: grc
aka: one another, each other, ἀλλήλων
match: reciprocal=“one another”
see: reflexive, pronoun
find: grc ^C-
short: The word for “one another”, ἀλλήλων. It is always plural.

ἀγαπᾶτε ἀλλήλους, “love one another” ([JHN 13:34]). Like a [[reflexive|reflexive]] pronoun it has no [[nominative]]: it is never the subject. It is one of the commonest words of the New Testament letters, which have much to say about how believers treat one another.

## demonstrative | Demonstrative pronoun
lang: both
aka: this, that, these, those, οὗτος, ἐκεῖνος, dem., pointing word
match: demonstrative=“this” or “that”: a pointing word; this/that=a word for “this” or “that”
see: pronoun, demonstrative-particle, greek-article
find: grc ^D-
short: A pointing word: “this”, “that”, “these”, “those”. In Greek, οὗτος is “this” and ἐκεῖνος is “that”.

οὗτος points to something near, or just mentioned: οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ υἱός μου, “this is my Son”. ἐκεῖνος points to something further away, or to someone other than the main person: “that day”, “he”.

A demonstrative next to a noun comes before “the” or after the noun, never between them: οὗτος ὁ ἄνθρωπος, “this man”. That is the opposite of an ordinary [[greek-adjective|adjective]]. Hebrew has demonstratives too: see [[demonstrative-particle]].

## relative | Relative pronoun
lang: grc
aka: who, which, that, whom, whose, ὅς, ἥ, ὅ, rel., relative clause
match: relative=“who”, “which”, “that”, joining a clause to a noun
see: pronoun, sentence-parts, hebrew-relative
find: grc ^R-
short: “Who”, “which” or “that”, introducing a clause that describes a noun: “the man *who* came”. In Greek: ὅς, ἥ, ὅ.

In “the word *which* I spoke”, “which” begins a clause describing “the word”. Greek's relative pronoun takes its [[gender]] and [[number]] from the noun it refers back to, but its [[case]] from its own job in its own clause: ὁ λόγος ὃν ἐλάλησα, “the word that I spoke” ([JHN 12:48]): ὅν is masculine like λόγος, and [[accusative]] because it is what was spoken.

Its forms look like the article's with a rough [[breathing|breathing]] and an accent: ὅς, ἥ, ὅ, οὗ, ᾧ, ὅν, ἥν. Don't confuse ἥ, “who” (feminine), with ἡ, “the”, or ἤ, “or”: the marks tell them apart. ὅστις is a related word meaning “whoever” or “who indeed”.

## interrogative | Interrogative: question words
lang: both
aka: who?, what?, why?, τίς, τί, interr., question word, interrogative pronoun
match: interrogative=a question word: who? what? why?; question word=a word that asks a question
see: indefinite, interrogative-particle, pronoun
find: grc ^I-
short: Question words: “who?”, “what?”, “why?”, “how?”. In Greek τίς is “who?” and τί is “what?” or “why?”.

τίς and τί always carry an acute accent (´) on the ι, which never turns into a grave (see [[accents]]). That is how you tell them apart from [[indefinite]] τις and τι, “someone” and “something”, which usually have none. Greek also has question adverbs and particles: πῶς, “how?”, ποῦ, “where?”, πότε, “when?”.

A question in Greek ends with ; which looks like an English semicolon: see [[greek-punctuation]]. In Hebrew a question can start with מִי, “who?”, מָה, “what?”, or the [[interrogative-particle|question prefix]] הֲ.

## indefinite | Indefinite pronoun
lang: grc
aka: someone, something, anyone, a certain, τις, τι
match: indefinite=“someone”, “something”, “a certain”
see: interrogative, pronoun
find: grc ^X- avoid=JHN2:5
short: τις and τι: “someone”, “something”, “a certain”. They look like τίς, “who?”, but usually have no accent.

With a noun, τις means “a certain”: ἄνθρωπός τις, “a certain man”. On its own it means “someone” or “anyone”: εἴ τις, “if anyone”. It rarely comes first in its clause, and it [[enclitic|leans on the word before it]], so it usually has no accent of its own, or passes one back to that word.

## correlative | Correlative pronoun
lang: grc
aka: correlative, how much, as many as, such as, ὅσος, οἷος, τοσοῦτος, πόσος, correl.
match: correlative=a word of amount or kind: “as much as”, “such as”, “so great”
see: relative, interrogative, pronoun
find: grc ^[KQ]-
short: Words for amount or kind that come in matching sets: ὅσος, “as much as”, τοσοῦτος, “so much”, οἷος, “such as”, πόσος, “how much?”.

These words come in families that answer each other: πόσος, “how much?”, τοσοῦτος, “so much”, ὅσος, “as much as”. STEPBible's labels call them correlatives, and call the [[interrogative|question words]] among them (such as πόσος) “correlative or interrogative”. They change their endings like adjectives.
